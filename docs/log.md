# The First Mate's Log

*Agreed 2026-11-13. Not built yet.*

Willie is promoted from deckhand to **first mate**: the job the player had under Bill (in the
tutorial: "First mate, actually."), now that the player is captain. He takes it very seriously,
and keeps the log. It is two things at once:

- **A record of the voyage**: what happened on the river, day by day, in Willie's hand. The
  record of *this* voyage, saved with it.
- **A novelette**: Willie's slow descent into **anachronitis**. See *The story* below.

## The story: a ship out of time

Found-document storytelling, Lovecraftian horror-comedy in shape (notes, mounting dread, a truth
too big to hold), wholesome in content. Willie is the sharpest eye aboard, the voyage's narrator,
and the joke is never on him: it's on the captain, which means the player, and us.

**Willie notices what doesn't fit.**

- **The band's music** is twenty years ahead of its time (like hearing Duran Duran in the
  mid-sixties), and Willie has an ear. The first clue.
- **Bennie's age:** the real Bennie Moten was born in late 1894. "Bennie claims to be born in
  '94, which would make him ten years old, younger than me, yet he is a man grown."
- **The game's own mechanics, as evidence:**
  - Skip: "The afternoon passed in a blink again. Gus says I dozed off. I did not doze off."
  - A reload: "I woke certain we had already passed Hog Island. We had not. Or we had, and then
    had not."
  - The map: "The Captain stares at the river as if from a great height."
  - Forks: "Again the Captain chose the lane with the crates in it, before anyone could have
    seen them."
- Later: the song "Steamboat Bill" whistled six years before it's written; Pretty Patty; the
  Thierry Belle; the bands' race; whatever else the voyage turns up.

**He takes it up with the Captain**, who offers explanations that make no sense inside the game
and perfect sense outside it. Willie writes them down faithfully, and worries.

**The pace:** slow. Chapter one has one or two small oddities, quickly explained away; the doubt
grows chapter by chapter. If he panics early, it stops being dread and becomes a gag.

**The ending (chapter five, the last stretch).** Willie confronts the Captain with the evidence.
The Captain laughs and explains everything: Willie is a character in a game inspired by a
century-old cartoon, itself a tribute to a silent film ("All movies are silent, Captain!":
moving pictures exist in 1904, but "silent" means nothing to him), itself after a folk song
recorded in 1910 (six years in the future!), itself after a famous steamboat race (the Robert E.
Lee and the Natchez, 1870, which Willie has heard of). All is revealed, and Willie concludes that
the Captain has lost his mind. He decides to stay, and help, and perhaps steer the Captain back to
sanity: loyalty over understanding.

**Always:** the Captain has no stated sex ("the Captain", all the way through).

## Three kinds of entry

- **Records:** terse, written automatically from what happens, a line each:
  - salvage: "Crate aboard: soggy cotton, $3."
  - soundings, in the leadsman's marks, not fathoms: "Sounding: mark three, clear water." (Mark
    three is three fathoms, eighteen feet; mark twain two fathoms, twelve feet, the safe least;
    quarter less three, a little under three.)
  - forks: "Advised the Captain: salvage to port of Horse Island." / "Kept the main channel past
    Horse Island."
  - encounters won or lost, purchases, wages, the weather, who came aboard.
- **Musings:** the novelette, written by us, triggered by story beats (the band first plays; the
  third Skip; a reload; reaching Dubuque) and by count. Longer entries.
- **Thoughts:** musings too long, or too private, for the header. They're written into the log but
  never typed out in the header's ticker: hidden gems, there for whoever opens the log. That's where
  Willie's inner life is: the header has the news as it happens, the log has the man. (The first:
  becoming First Mate, a little way out of Muscatine.)

## Where it's read

1. **The header** (over the voyage's pages and the river): the day's heading, "Day 2: Muscatine,
   upbound", and under it **the latest entry**, cut off with "…" when it's long. Tap to open the
   reading view.
2. **The reading view** overlays the map with good contrast, while **the river keeps going behind
   it**:
   - new records append at the bottom as they happen;
   - minor encounters resolve on their own while you read and are logged; major ones (The
     Sandbar, the rapids) close the log and come up as usual;
   - **the two buttons stay** at the bottom, so a fork can still be answered (and Willie logs it).
3. **Days:** each day is a page; page back through the voyage (tabs or arrows).

The log is saved with the voyage.

## The look

- **Special Elite** (the typewriter face we already have): Willie types the log.
- **The header types itself:** a new entry appears in the header character by character, in real
  time, as if being typed: that's what draws the eye to the log, and to the reading view.
- **The reading view doesn't:** what's there is there. Only an entry written while it's open, and in
  view, types itself out.

**A later musing: the typewriter itself.** By 1904 typewriters were common in offices (the
Remington, the Underwood), but dear (a hundred dollars, months of a deckhand's wages), heavy, and
no thing for a riverboat; the light portables came later (the Corona, 1912). Yet here is Willie,
typing out every sounding and every sighting. Where did it come from? Who paid for it? Why is the
ribbon never dry?
