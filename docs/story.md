## The premise

**Every encounter ends in a game of Snag Poker.** It is silly, and it is a
proud tradition: in a fighting game every meeting ends in a fight, in an
action game the story sets up the shooting, the sneaking or the race, a
superhero film sets up its set pieces, and Might and Magic: Clash of Heroes
has characters and story galore that always end in a match-three puzzle. On
our river, Snag Poker is simply how things are settled: by harbour masters,
miners, inspectors and rival captains alike. Camp, played straight.

**A storytale, on screen.** The game never gives a date or a year: it's the age of steam, and it begins on the first day of spring, which is what they call the day the ice goes out. The deadline is the day the World's Fair opens in St. Louis. Anyone curious can work out the year from that; the fable is better without it. The dates in these notes are background, for us.

**A 1927 cartoon about the spring of 1904.** The look is the mid-1920s; the
story is set in April 1904. That's no contradiction: it's a cartoon made in
1927 looking back on the good old days of steamboats and straw boaters, the
way the films of the 1920s looked back on the Gay Nineties. Its voice is fond,
a little knowing, and free with the facts.

**The deadline is the World's Fair.** The Louisiana Purchase Exposition in
St. Louis opened on 30 April 1904 (to verify), and automobiles were on show
there: the horseless carriage has to be there for opening day. Gentle
pressure, never a threat.

**No villain, no conflict to speak of.** This is not the Odyssey. It's about
fulfilling obligations: getting the cargo to where the boss has been paid to
deliver it, and every other job taken on along the way. Nobody is chasing the
boat, nobody hires card sharps to stop it (Henry Ford is not sabotaging
anything), and the captains you beat bear no grudge: everyone you meet would
rather like to win the hand, and is a good sport when they don't. It isn't even
the end of the world if you never reach St. Louis.

**Touchstones:** *Three Men in a Boat* (1889), a comic river journey of
episodes, digressions and eccentrics, without a villain or real stakes;
*Around the World in 80 Days* for the delivery and the deadline (minus its
detective); *Kiki's Delivery Service* for the young courier making their way
among strangers, a job at a time, kindness and a dip in confidence instead of
a villain; *Paddington* (the 2014 film) for the newcomer and a cast of
eccentrics won over, but drier, not so twee; *Silver Linings Playbook* for
messy, likeable people with troubles of their own, a family that bickers
and means well, a wager riding on the outcome, and a contest at the end
where the score that counts is a modest one, not first place.
Huckleberry Finn has the river, but more danger than we want.
A cozy road movie on water: the tension is small, practical and comic.
Obligations pile up (snags as cargo, jobs taken on with a handshake), things
go off (the eggs, the ice), there's a queue at the lock.

**What follows for the game:**

- **Losing is never punishing.** Lose an encounter and you pay something (a
  sum, a detour, an extra snag, a day) and the voyage goes on. No game over on
  the river; not reaching St. Louis is just a different, sillier ending.
- **Opponents are acquaintances, not enemies.** They come back, remember you
  fondly, want a rematch, owe you or you owe them.
- **Each landing is a short reel:** a vignette with its own set-up, its own
  little twist on the game, and a punchline as you leave. Written one at a
  time, together, the way the hands lesson was.

## Principles

- **Captions are a literary form.** Every line is written, read aloud, and
  turned over before it goes in. No filler, no generated padding. Fewer, better
  lines beat many average ones.
- **Short and punchy,** like the silent films: one sentence, two at most,
  plain words, a period flavour, a joke when one is earned.
- **Never in the way.** Every caption can be dismissed; none blocks a decision
  for longer than it takes to read.
- **The game teaches; the story motivates.** Rules come through play, not
  through text walls.
- **No feature creep.** A mechanic goes in when an encounter we actually want
  needs it, not before.

## The story

**Muscatine.** The old captain, **Steamboat Bill** (everyone aboard calls him
Captain Bill; the player is no relation, just his trusted First Mate), has just landed a big job: deliver a horseless carriage to the World's
Fair in St. Louis. That is how fin-de-siècle logistics work. The carriage
waits upriver in Dubuque: a short run up to fetch it, and then it is all
downriver, an easy passage.

Then family calls him ashore (someone to tend to; between cargo and family,
family wins). The First Mate, the player, is made captain: *"You're the captain
now. Just stay away from those whiskey-swilling river gamblers and their Snag
Poker!"*

Except the passage isn't easy. There are snags everywhere. The harbour master
won't clear the boat unless you beat him at Snag; the captains ahead in the
queue at the bridge works will trade their place for a hand; the mail bag goes
with whoever wins it. Coal moves the boat, but Snag Poker moves the river.

So the young captain breaks the promise to Bill at the first landing, and
Morag's ("You should NEVER play for money!") soon after, and keeps breaking
them all the way to St. Louis.

**No villains.** Nobody is chasing the boat, nobody wants it sunk. It is
river life, and poker is a large part of it. Every encounter is a transaction:
passage, funds, a place in the queue, a job taken on or handed off.

**The goal: honour your obligations.** Reach the Fair with the carriage, and
every other job you took on along the way delivered or passed on fair and
square. Then the Fair's great river tournament opens, and the League
begins.

## The cargo: Pretty Patty

The horseless carriage is a prototype, the only one of its kind, crated on
deck. Its makers call it something grand and patented; the crew call it
**Pretty Patty**. The irony stays unstated: the boat is carrying the
instrument of its own undoing. In a few decades small wheels on tarmac will
take over from the river, and the ambling life of landings and steamboats
will go with them. Nobody aboard suspects; the cartoon of 1927 knows, and
says nothing.

**Its maker is fictional, its flavour real.** A river-town workshop, an
eccentric inventor, one machine built, and a reputation riding on it reaching
the Palace of Transportation. Period cars went by the maker's name and the
word "patent"; the full name is still to settle (Pettibone's Patent Motor
Buggy was one idea). Real background to borrow from:

- **Adams-Farwell of Dubuque, Iowa**, upriver from Muscatine, made cars from
  1903, with a rotary engine whose cylinders spun round a crankshaft standing
  still. A cartoon engine: it whirls of its own accord. (Whether they showed
  at the Fair: to verify.)
- **The rival delivery.** In the summer of 1904 the AAA ran a tour of
  motorcars from New York, Philadelphia and Baltimore to the Fair, about 1,350
  miles in 18 days, to prove cars could manage America's roads; it began the
  Glidden Tours. Dozens of cars driving in overland, and one floating down
  the river.
- **The Palace of Transportation** was the Fair's second-largest exhibit
  hall: Benz, Daimler and Panhard & Levassor showed cars there, beside
  locomotives.

**A Fair a year late.** The Louisiana Purchase was made in 1803: Napoleon,
his Caribbean empire lost with the failed expedition to Saint-Domingue and
war with Britain about to resume, sold the territory for $15 million. The
centennial Fair was meant for 1903 and was put off to 1904, to let more
states and nations take part, and because the building ran late. A story
about racing to reach it on time can let that lie, or wink at it once.

## Snags as cargo

In the League a snag is a penalty to pass on. In the story it is cargo and
duty: the mail bag, the stinking hides, the eggs that must not go rotten, the
horseless carriage itself. It works the same at the table, and reads
differently:

- **Upkeep is the cost of carrying it:** feed for the livestock, ice for the
  eggs.
- **Pawning it** is another captain taking the job off your hands, which is
  the mechanic we already have.
- **Delivering it** is new: at the right landing (or simply "in St. Louis"),
  the snag leaves your hand and the obligation is met.
- **A full hand of snags is a full hold,** and a reason to take a detour or
  play someone for their place.
- Some snags are **reputation**, not cargo: decline the captain's code and you
  are handed *Yellow-belly* for free. It goes when you pass it on, like any
  snag.

**The kinds of snag in the story,** all played the same way at the table
(win a showdown with a snag played, and the weakest hand takes it):

1. **Cargo and duty:** the mail, the eggs, Patty. Delivered, or handed on.
2. **Reputation:** Yellow-belly. Lived down, by passing it on.
3. **Bills** are duties with a price: whoever holds the Dock Fee when the
   game ends pays it (a snag you can pawn on the very man you owe).
4. **Troubles:** what the river leaves behind (a Broken Pole, Mud in the
   Boiler, a Lost Day, a Sprained Back). Each costs upkeep (coal, wages,
   time) until shed. Shedding them is the evening's guile: in cartoon logic,
   whoever loses to you agrees to fix your boiler or lend you a pole.

## The river at the table

The river's hazards don't happen to you: they sit down and play. A
personified Sandbar, Fog Bank or sheet of ice is very 1927 (the Silly
Symphonies are full of weather with faces), and it makes an ordeal of
shovels and ropes into a hand of cards, because this is a poker game. Nobody
has to lose for you to win: it's you against the river.

- **Each hazard plays to its nature,** with play styles the engine already
  has: **the Sandbar** stands pat on everything (stubborn); **the Fog Bank**
  plays half blind (dreamer); **the Runaway Mule** throws its cards away
  (anarchist).
- **Its stack is the obstacle, not money: sand** (or ice, soggy lumber,
  flotsam and chaff). The Sandbar takes your coin and turns it into sand:
  you pour in coal and man-hours, and get nothing back but sandbags. Fleece
  it to get off: every pot you take washes some of it away.
- **Time is the price.** A day has a travel allowance (some 8 hours under
  way); every hand against the Sandbar eats into it, and what's left is the
  distance made. Fleece it quickly and you still make the landing; slowly,
  and you tie up short for the night, somewhere along the bank, with
  whoever else is stuck there for company (or nobody).
- **Snags against the river.** The first idea was that snags go one way: the
  Sandbar cares nothing for your In-Laws or the Mail Run. The game doesn't work
  that way: you can play your snags at an obstacle, and if you win they go to it,
  where it may throw them back. We keep that, for now. It starts
  with a chunk of sand and a few troubles (a Twisted Axle, a Broken Paddle),
  and when it wins a showdown they're yours, to be fixed later. Coming off
  clean is the best ending; carrying a Broken Paddle to the next landing is
  the usual one.
- **There's nothing to win but freedom.** Win a pot and you get back only
  what you put in; the sand in it simply drifts off downstream (to make a
  sandbar somewhere else). Lose one and everything you put in turns to sand
  on its pile. Against the river you never come out ahead, only lose less,
  and the battle still has to be fought.
- **Stuck isn't over.** Run out of money or hours before the sand runs out,
  and you're stuck: no landing tonight. The night's game aboard, with the
  crew and passengers, builds your purse (your effort) back up, and you try
  again in the morning. Or another boat comes by and drags you off, if you
  take some of its snags, or poker it into it.

**Formats:**

- **The Sandbar, heads-up:** fleece it, against the clock.
- **The Ice Jam (High Rollers):** early April, ice let go upstream jammed at a narrows:
  several sheets of ice at one table, each its own seat, to be played clear of one by
  one. They play as one, pieces of one jam: see LeClaire to Dubuque.
- **Two boats on one bar:** the player and a stuck captain both at the
  Sandbar's table. Beat it together and both float free: helping, not a
  contest of who stays stuck.

**How it fits the engine.** Mostly as it is: the obstacle is a seat, its
sand a purse; antes and fees go into the pot as now; its troubles are snags
it plays when it wins, and the weakest hand (you) takes them, as now; it
plays the stubborn style (stands pat, never gives up). What's new:

1. **One-sided settling.** A hand settles as each seat's share of the pot
   less what it put in. Against an obstacle: if you win, you get back only
   your own stake and its sand vanishes; if it wins, your whole stake becomes
   sand on its pile. One rule at the end of the hand; the hand itself is
   untouched.
2. **Snags:** first planned as one way, with no check built. Your snags can be
   played at an obstacle like at anyone (see above).
3. **Troubles:** a new set of snags (Twisted Axle, Broken Paddle, Busted
   Boiler, Fatigue, Soggy Lumber…), their upkeep the cost of the repair
   until it's done.
4. **The ending:** won when its pile is gone; stuck when your purse or the
   day's hours run out (a hand an hour).
5. **The picture:** coins flying into the pot and turning to sand on its
   side; the sand drifting off downstream when you win; ice, flotsam or
   lumber for the others. The coin flights already know who pays whom: this
   is drawing, not logic.

*Tuning:* its pile only shrinks when it puts sand in and loses, so a
stubborn obstacle that always calls keeps feeding the pot, and the pile's
size sets how long the fight lasts: four to six hands, perhaps, an ordeal
without a drag.

## Where snags go

Story snags aren't only passed on: many have somewhere they belong, and
getting them there resolves them for good.

**Troubles have a fixer.** The deck game aboard, crew and passengers round a
lantern (stranded on a bar, or moored at a landing with no saloon), is a
different game from the landing's: a ceremony of resilience and community.
It doesn't matter who gets a snag, or who wins the hand: lay a trouble on the
table and it leaves you. Whoever takes it comments, they talk over who's the
better match (*"That's one for Gus, Captain"*), and pass it among themselves.
By morning it's fixed and gone. The Busted Boiler and Twisted Axle end up
with Gus, the Broken Pole and Paddle with Lars, Fatigue and the Broken Heart
with Willie, whose good nature is indomitable.

**Cargo and passengers have an address.** The Mail Run goes to a town; the
In-Laws are going somewhere; the Runaway Mule belongs in Podunk Junction.
Stopping there, even as a detour, resolves the snag: delivered, seen home.
Which makes the route a choice: is the detour worth it, or do you play the
snag onto someone else?

**And snags help, on the river.** They're your snags, after all. Once a
day, each one can dodge you a bullet: lose a pot to an obstacle, and a snag
gives you back what you just lost, and is spent for the day. You don't play
it; it **volunteers** (the mule hauls, the In-Laws push, the preacher
prays). So every snag is a cost at the landing games (upkeep) and insurance
on the river, and carrying a few is a real choice. See the mule home and
it's gone: no more upkeep, and no more mule.

**Each snag has its own temper:** which hands of the day it helps in. A day
against an obstacle runs to some eight hands, the stakes rising as it goes
(the last are the dearest to lose). A volunteer gives back every pot lost in
its window. One rule: **people and animals volunteer; objects, obligations
and troubles don't.** First drafts:

| Snag | Helps in hands | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **The In-Laws** | 1 | Supportive but elderly: they help with the cheapest loss of the day, then they're all tuckered out. |
| **The Layabout Nephew** | 1 (or 8) | Lends a hand at the start, before he finds a place to lie down. (Or: sleeps all day, turns up for the last hand, terribly pleased with himself.) |
| **The Travelling Preacher** | 3 | Appeals to higher powers, on the third (the Trinity). Perhaps also a **blessing**: every other volunteer works one hand longer. |
| **The Unionized Coal Shoveller** | 3–6 | The union keeps turning up, but takes a while to get going, and goes home at the whistle. |
| **The Runaway Mule** | 6–8 | Stubborn and temperamental, and strong: won't go first, but once he starts he sticks it out to the end of the day, kicking back every loss when the stakes are highest. |
| **The Crate of Live Chickens** | one, at random | Chickens. |
| The Mail Run, the Unpaid Coal Bill | – | Obligations. |
| The Rotten Eggs, the Stinking Hides | – | Things. |
| Troubles (Busted Boiler…) | – | Broken things; they need fixing, not thanks. |

*The Sandbar and the Fog Bank* are snags in the League, but in the story
they're obstacles at the table, not cards in your hand.

Knowing your windows is the strategy: in a covered hand a loss costs
nothing, so that's the hand to push hard and wash the sand away fastest.
Two volunteers covering the same hand is a waste (one loss, one helper);
spreading them across the day is the art. Balance by upkeep: a strong
volunteer is a dear one to carry to the landings.

**All of a snag in one place.** Each snag's nature (what kind it is, its
temper, its fixer, its address, what it costs) lives on the snag's own
definition, one table for the set, and the engine reads it from there: no
"if it's the mule" scattered through the code. See design.md.

## Encounters

Every meeting, the tutorial included, is a match with its own settings plus a
script:

| Setting | Options |
|---|---|
| Who | One captain, or a table of several |
| Pot | Off, or on |
| Currency | Coins, or buttons (the tutorial) |
| Deck | Shuffled, or arranged hand by hand |
| Format | Until someone's broke, a set number of hands, best of three |
| Stakes | The ante, plus whatever a reply adds ("I'll put $5 in the pot") |
| Script | The conversation and its triggers |

Story mode is a chain of encounters, and flags decide which lines play.

**Only the gates block the way.** Within a stretch of river (see The river
in segments) you can come and go: an encounter left unsolved waits for you.
A gate (the rapids, the lock) must be solved to pass. What "solved" means is
the encounter's own: usually winning, sometimes not (see the coal miners).

## The caption system

**The player speaks in a word.** As in Morag's lessons, your answers on the
buttons are short (*Gus?*, *Snag?*, *Willie?*), and tapping one spells it
out in a caption (*"Why not Mr. Klemm? No one knows the ship better."*). The
button stays quick to read; the caption gives the player a voice. Written
out only where it adds something: a plain *Why?* is enough as it is.

**Sizes** (built): an **aside** over the speaker's cards, play going on
underneath; a **scene** from their cards down to the board; the **full**
table but the buttons, for introductions and lessons.

**Speakers** (built). The opponent's lines have the notch pointing up at their
portrait. Anyone else (Willie, Morag from the rail, the player) has their
name above the quote and their portrait in the corner of the caption.

**Closing** (built), in any combination:

- **Auto-close:** a countdown ring runs round the speaker's portrait; when it
  is full, the caption goes.
- **Manual:** tap the caption.
- **Replies:** one or two buttons with labels of their own (defaults *Agree* /
  *Decline*). Each leads to another caption, or to an effect. A single reply
  sits on the white side; the black one stays empty, with no pretend "No".
- **Compliance:** the caption waits for a move at the table. *"Throw away
  that four, child"*: the button stays disabled until you have tapped the four
  and only the four, and making the move closes the caption.

**Effects,** a short list that grows only when an encounter needs it: start
the match; add to the pot; the opponent folds before the deal (no ante paid);
give a snag; deliver a snag; turn the pot on (or the buttons into coins); set
or clear a flag; move or adjust money (IOUs).

**Flags.** A plain list of strings in the save, pushed when something starts
and removed when it resolves (`owesGaylord`, `promisedMorag`). No ledger
screen, no new UI.

**To build, from the tutorial play-through:**

- **Heavy buttons** (built, `src/ui/heavy.ts`). Only the moves that commit you
  to the hand (Call, Swap, Play hand) are held; everything else (Fold, Check,
  Skip, Next, Back, replies) is a light tap: giving up, or moving on, takes no
  time at all (← on the keyboard for the left button). Hold → or Space or Return for the white; `?quick` in
  development turns holding off. Each press soaks its own paper. A choice should feel decisive, not a brush against
  glass: hold to commit (0.9 s). The button soaks up ink like thin paper
  pressed into an inkpad, from a stylised fingerprint where the finger
  presses; let go early and it drains back, but the fingerprint lingers,
  fading: a patina on the controls. A haptic tick at the commit where phones
  allow it; Enter or Space held the same; screen readers activate at once.
  Only the action buttons; tapping cards and captions stays light.
- **Longer replies** (built: any number of replies; more than two, or any
  too long for half the row on this screen, stack up from the buttons, the
  last, white, nearest the thumb). Some replies don't fit half a row (*"No, ma'am"* is
  cut off on phones). A single reply may take the full width; and some
  conversations may want more than two replies, stacked. A compact game:
  desktop needn't look its best.

**How the player is addressed** is just text, by speaker and mood: Captain,
Cap'n, Herr Kapitän; Ellie's "Captain of my heart".

## Worked exchanges

### The stinking hides (replies, a branch, the captain's code)

1. A captain wants rid of a cargo of hides. *Agree* / *Decline*.
2. Decline → the player: *"Never! Your stinking hides would foul my ship."*
3. The captain: *"Captain's code, then. Beat me at Snag and I keep them; lose
   and they're yours. I'll put $5 in the pot."* *Agree* / *Decline*.
4. Agree → the match starts, $5 extra in the pot.
5. Decline → *"You refuse the code? Good day, then."* They fold before the
   deal (no ante), and the player is handed **Yellow-belly** (*does not honour
   the captain's code*).

### Gaylord's sponsorship (an obligation that comes back)

- Bored and flush, Gaylord invests "before the money is gone anyway": he pays
  the young captain's entry into a big table (a multi-captain game) for an IOU,
  $50 now, $100 back. Not a snag: an obligation (flag `owesGaylord`).
- Later he turns up to claim it: perhaps an expensive extension if the
  player's purse is thin, perhaps a best-of-three to settle it, or, destitute
  himself by then, asking for a loan on the same terms.
- Not adversarial, not generous either. The same shape serves other stories:
  promising Morag to look for Finlay, getting caught up in one of Ellie's
  schemes, or enlisting Max Moritz against the Walrus and the Inspector.

## Morag, the Governess

- A governess by trade and by temperament, on the river looking for her
  husband, **Finlay**, who has slipped off to play.
- She **loves** Snag Poker and is a master of it. What she cannot abide is
  everything that comes with it: the whisky, the wagering, the company.
- Author of a pamphlet on the subject (a candidate title: *On the Vileness of
  River Gaming*), which she quotes when it suits her.
- As a tutor: exacting, dry, secretly fond of a good pupil. Praise from her is
  rare and therefore worth something.
- She **is** the tutorial. In the story she probably returns as a passenger
  on the player's boat (name to be found), chasing Finlay downriver,
  disapproving all the way, quietly proud of you at the Fair.

## Landings (a dozen to aim for; six good ones will do)

Formats to mix: heads-up until the pot is won, best of three, table games.
Reasons to mix: to pass on a snag, to get passage, to get funds, to take on a
job.

**The route: up, then back down.** From Muscatine up to Dubuque for Pretty
Patty, then all the way down to St. Louis. Going up is the setup: slow
against the current and hungry for coal, the deck empty, Morag's lessons,
promises made and jobs taken on with a handshake. Coming down is the payoff:
Patty aboard, the current helping, and the same landings again, where the
people met on the way up remember you: debts called in, favours returned,
jobs to deliver. Two visits for one setting, as long as the second visit
changes something (the risk is that the way back feels like padding).

*Upriver:*

1. **Muscatine.** Bill ashore; the Dock Fee, paid or played for with the
   harbour master.
2. **The Tri-Cities** (Davenport, Rock Island, Moline). In spring 1904 nobody
   agrees how many cities they are: Gilbert has only just renamed itself
   **Bettendorf** (1903, after the brothers whose wagon works it lured from
   Davenport), and East Moline was incorporated the same year; "Quad Cities"
   only won out around 1930. So the local game runs until someone wins with
   **trips**, or with **quads**, depending on which side of the argument the
   table takes: the old guard plays to three, Bettendorf to four (and in
   Bettendorf they'll bet on anything, including which it'll be). Quads
   should stay a legend, not a requirement: a game played strictly until four
   of a kind could go on all night. The **railroad bridge** at Rock Island
   (the first across the Mississippi, 1856; Lincoln defended it in court
   after a steamboat hit it: from memory) is the queue: a line of boats, play
   the captains ahead for their place.
3. **The coal depot, and the striking miners** (below). Rock Island County
   was coal country: **Coal Valley**, a few miles south of Moline, was named
   for its mines, open from the 1850s and worked until 1942, all
   shallow room-and-pillar diggings in the Rock Island seam. The depot on the
   Rock Island or Moline levee, fed from Coal Valley (how the coal reached the
   river: to verify), struck. The boat burns through coal going upstream, so
   this is where it bites.
4. **LeClaire and the Rock Island Rapids.** Above the Tri-Cities, some 14
   miles of rock ledges; boats took on a specialist rapids pilot at LeClaire,
   a town that lived by the trade (from memory, to verify). **Ellie Sawyer**,
   the best pilot on the rapids, picks her clients at Snag Poker: she seats

## The Pilot's Tournament (replaces the waiting table)

**The real rapids.** The Rock Island Rapids ran some 14 miles from **LeClaire
at the head** down to **Rock Island at the foot**, falling about 20 feet at
low water, every rock and chain named after a wreck. LeClaire lived by its
rapids pilots, forty or fifty of them waiting on the bank; the Green Tree
Hotel at the head was their waiting room. Rapids City sits across from
LeClaire on the Illinois bank, Port Byron just north of it, Cordova a few
miles on. The dams came later: the Moline Lock in 1907, the LeClaire canal in
the 1920s, Lock and Dam 15 drowned the rapids by 1934, and 13 and 14 date from
the 1930s, so today's map no longer shows them (the rough water at Fulton
is something else). So a boat bound upriver waits at the foot, and LeClaire is where it arrives.

**The chains, going up** (W. Blair's *On the River*, 1932, an old rapids man's
list). From the bridge draw at Davenport north to LeClaire: the Lower chain and
Stubbs eddy; the **Moline chain** and Brazil Rock (after the steamer *Brazil*);
the **Duck Creek chain** and Mason's Rock (the *A. G. Mason*, 1856); Winnebago
Island (the *Silas Wright*); **Campbell's chain** (the *Danube*, the *Louisville*,
the *Little Eagle*); Hampton, where deep water comes closest to shore; the **St.
Louis chain** and **Cabin chain**, nearly continuous (the *St. Louis* was one of
the river's heaviest losses; the *Mary C.*, 1856); **Sycamore**, a limestone
ledge (the *Emerald*, the *Envoy*, the *J. K. Graves*, 1894); then **Smith's
chains** and the **Upper chain**: with St. Louis and Cabin, the longest and hardest
stretch on the river. Pilots steered by lining up the jackstaff on shore marks,
held a set distance, then turned for the next mark, all the way to LeClaire.
Pilots worked from Davenport and Rock Island as well as LeClaire.

**The climb: three stages, six loads.** Each push at a stage burns a load of
coal, and the crew come to it with their full strength. Stages can only be worn
down: what you lose goes down the river, so preparation counts. The Moline Chain
(15, one snag), Campbell's Chain (30, three), and after Hampton's breather the
Long Reach (60, six). After a failed push Gus says how much of it you took, and
you may **push again** or **turn back** with the coal that's left, to the back of
the queue. Out of coal, you're swept back to Davenport all the same. Not every
player will get past the rapids, and that's all right. The way through is to lose
slowly: antes rise, so whoever lasts takes the biggest bites. Simulated for a
careful captain, through in six loads: strength 18, 22%; 26, 34%; 34, 58%; 44, 84%.

**It's on the Captain.** Gus states it plainly all along, matter of fact, never
complaining: *"We can't climb the rapids with a single fireman, Captain, even one as
strong as Lars."* An engine overhaul, another fireman, a professional cook.
Willie apologises for the beans. Rumours on the deck: the *Mathilda* came back
down by evening, cargo lost; the *Terry Belle* is back in the queue. Lenny sings
it at Bettendorf: *everybody knows the thaw's too strong.* Then, at the foot of the
rapids, *"Just give me all she's got, Mr. Klemm." "It does not work like that,
Captain."* The Mercantile sells a boiler and an engine overhaul, each doubling that
station's work. Below 34 strength, he won't let it drop. And when it goes wrong,
no I-told-you-so: *"We'll do better next time, Captain."* He'd do anything to climb
those rapids, and now you know his hints were right.

**The Tri-Cities are the hub.** The queue is at **Davenport**, at the foot of
the rapids, and the whole area is a place to live a while: losing money to
the miners at Rock Island, selling coal at a profit while it's short, ferrying
goods, passengers and livestock across and up and down. You beat the Sandbar
to get here, so working up funds and skill before going on is no hardship.

**The tournament.** The spring thaw: one pilot crazy enough to take boats up
(she prefers *adventurous*), coming down from Cordova around noon and taking
one boat up in the afternoon. Boats line up, and Snag picks who goes.

1. **Evening before:** moor; the deck game, where Willie has the lay of the
   land.
2. **Morning, $5:** three tables of captains, each to the last one with money;
   the table takes all the entries. Yours is played; the others are simulated,
   and we simply meet whoever won. Normal rules: no trips or quads.
3. **Noon at the saloon, $10:** the three winners and the pilot, who is upfront
   about her name and the prize. Win, or be runner-up if she wins. Four $10
   entries: the pot never passes $40.
4. Up the rapids, or wait for tomorrow.

**No walkover.** She takes the winner, and that's River Code: stuck here a
week, then that's a week of poker. Players can leave and practise in the
League or the tutorial. Losing days pass in odd jobs off screen, enough to
fund the next entry (*"You ferry a herd of cattle across the river, and spend
the evening cleaning up the manure."*). After a good while a second pilot
appears, **Lindy Huffer**, who just takes the highest bidder.

**The river doesn't wait for you.** Characters keep their own calendar, by Days
gone, whatever the player does: you walk in on whatever's happening, or hear
about it later. Nobody is waiting for the Captain.

**Gaylord's week.** Ellie ran the first games on the first day of spring. On day
2 Gaylord, who heard over in Bettendorf, won on behalf of a client. He fills his
week with clients (rich captains of big companies, who could sue).

| Day | At the noon table |
|---|---|
| 2 | Gaylord wins for a client; Ellie is puzzled |
| 3 | She's catching on (the earliest a player can be there) |
| 4 | Threats |
| 5 | Contracts, lawyers, *"Three more."* The Bingo ultimatum: *"Code says game. It doesn't say poker."* She'll bring her auntie down from Cordova |

**Bettendorf Breakfast** is on the morning list while he's in town: a throwaway
*"Gaylord Montague is staying at the saloon"*, and $4 to treat the crew (+4
strength tomorrow). And look, there's Gaylord, what a coincidence. It's where he
says whatever he'd say to you. *Ten dollars* covers his breakfast, stay and
entry; he takes the noon entry out of the winnings and keeps the rest. He's all
but booked: sign on days 3 to 5 and he plays your seat on day 8, the last of the
week, so hiring him is no speed run. Not if you owe him. You watch from your own seat, tapping I see, as he talks shop.
He can be in two places in a slot: the player can't, so it never shows.

**His loan: $50**, double back when you next meet in two weeks, doubled again
if the third week begins. He only brings it up with someone who has beaten him
at a table, any table, and who isn't a client. Otherwise he never mentions it,
so the player has no reason to know it exists. Take it and he won't play for
you. He despises anyone bad at poker, or who lacks the conviction even to lose
gracefully, which means his own clients; past day 8 he says so at the saloon.
From about day 7 he
asks after passage to St. Louis, and offers to play you for it; he leaves on a
cruiser around day 21.

**Bettendorf, coming down** (after Dubuque, not on the first visit; until
then a game there ends on trips). His presence has changed the game: it's quads
now, and it runs until a quad or someone's broke. It's a grind: few will sit in
with him now that they've seen how the rule skews the odds, and the Bettendorfers
are betting on how long until he leaves. The IOU is a snag that can
only be played at his table, and he can play it back; a quad ends it. He wants
passage to St. Louis, and plays you for the fare.

**Finlay** is a Tri-Cities background carouser: bet-crazy, enthusiastic, not
very good.

**The docking menu.** Every place you arrive at is the same screen: the boat,
the status line, a word from Gus or Willie, and a list. Each entry opens a
similar menu or starts a game (or a greeting before one). Cities have many
entries, small landings one or two. There's always a free anchorage: downstream
going up, upstream coming down. The Tri-Cities: anchor downstream, Bettendorf
Saloon, Moline Mercantile (the docking fee: chandler, market halls, dock
hands), Rock Island Coal Depot, Davenport Pilot Games (mornings, after Willie's
intel, 6 coal), Odd Jobs (a list of its own, free to open).

**Three slots a day:** morning, afternoon, evening. Everything fills one: the
depot, the mercantile, a job. The Pilot Games are mornings only and fill the
day. The evening is the deck game at anchor, or Bettendorf instead (the crew
stay aboard, playing among themselves and listening to Lars sing).

**Coal, and what nobody tells the player.** Coal is dear on the black market
($10 to buy, $8 to sell), so a job that burns a load pays next to nothing: the
free market adjusts, and the US Mail doesn't, so mail always sits undelivered.
But a full bunker from the depot costs what you lost there, about $2 a load. So
the mail pays after all, for anyone with a friend at the depot; and better
still, lose at the depot, sell on the black market, and repeat, as the days tick
by. Nobody says so. Willie only remarks that at these prices it doesn't pay.
Spring is eternal here: no pressure but the day count. Antes go by the table,
not the day, so waiting never makes the game dearer.

**The depot, friendly.** *"Been a while! Ain't seen The Best Intentions since
last summer. Where's Bill?"* No bad feelings and no giveaways: a game to pass
the time, with the union's strike chest on the table. Win or lose they're
delighted, and bankrupting them changes nothing (the union sends more). They
hand out Pamphlet Distribution and the Strike Support Button, and take on any
snag you play them: they've got the time. Lose enough, and some sacks have
fallen off the heap by your boat.

**The council, reworked.** One paragraph of advice, not everyone in turn
(Lars's notes take up the screen). The strategic choices live here: odd jobs,
see the gambler, a short freight job Willie has found, Gus on purchases and
repairs.

**The Best Intentions page.** How beaten up she is; passenger rooms
(Storage, Cabin, Luxury); coal, spares, goods in the hold (sold at any
landing, at its prices), extra crew. Combined with the settings, opened by the
gently turning paddle wheel in place of the cogwheel.

**Days gone: X.** The day count, a tribute to *Days Gone*.

## Three kinds of chapter

- **A passage:** a stretch of river. Some exposition, something happens on the
  water, you arrive. Muscatine and the Sandbar.
- **A hub:** a place to potter about: a docking menu, jobs, a shop, a table,
  regulars keeping their own calendar. The Tri-Cities. You stay as long as you like.
- **A gate:** an announced obstacle with real risk. A game to earn the attempt, then
  the ordeal; fail, and you're back at the hub. The rapids. Gates work both ways: the
  same gate going down, with its own game (Rapids City).

**Each teaches a way of playing, not only a place.** The Sandbar: win pots to
wear it down. The Tri-Cities table: hunt a big hand. The depot: lose on purpose,
at a showdown. The Games: last one standing. The rapids: lose slowly. A new
chapter should ask for something the last ones didn't.

## Up is power, down is control

The voyage is a metaphor, and industry's too. Going up is about power: boats that
fail just don't get where they want to be. Going down is much faster and much
more dangerous, about control, mitigating danger and repairing damage: it's on the
downswing that boats sink. They cease to exist. Boom and bust; coal will follow
timber, and the river trade both. Hubs on the way down are for restoration, not
upgrades. Ideas: the boat's condition as the thing spent, a speed choice each leg,
Patty's own condition, obstacles that strike rather than block.

## LeClaire to Dubuque (about 83 miles)

After the rapids, easy going: not everything is a mad dash.

- **A drifting raft:** a game, gossip, leisure. Rafters live aboard, in houseboats
  and tents on the logs: a floating village.
- **Clinton:** a boomtown gone bust. Lumber peaked 1880–94, 13 or 17 millionaires by
  the sources, Lamb, Young, Joyce, Curtis; essentially finished by 1900, the barons
  gone south and west, a few mills left (the last closed in 1910). Thousands left
  (how many is disputed). Less business than hoped, and no demand but for booze.
- **Towing the raft folk:** rafts only come down, so this is people, not logs:
  their floating homes, all the way to Dubuque. See The tow, below.
- **Sabula:** coal, but dear: the top-up that spares the rafts. Not yet an island in
  1904: that came with Lock and Dam 13 in the 1930s. The railroad bridge to Savanna has a swing
  span: built 1881 (one source says 1882), replaced 1906, so in 1904 it's the older
  iron one. A whistle-timing moment: signal the tender in time, or drift for the
  piers. Whistles as river signals are real; the rest is game logic.
- **The Ice Jam:** a table of three ice sheets, with quips and puns. See below.
- **Bellevue:** a leadsman calling depths past an invented hazard. Don't name a
  ledge: no source for one. See The Deadheads, below.
- **The last leg to Dubuque.** The echo trick, finding your way by whistle off the
  bluffs: no source, but lovely, and a use for the whistle in the Fog Bank. The Julien
  Dubuque monument on the bluff as you arrive: 25-foot limestone tower, dedicated
  1897, over a grave from 1810. The Mines of Spain were still worked in 1904 (mining
  ran to 1914), so a camp or shanty boats are fair colour.

**Don't use:** Sabula as "Iowa's only island city" (not until the 1930s); Clinton's
rafts clogging the river (the 1902 Herald says few rafts, the trade dying); the
Savanna Army depot (about 1917 as we recall it, unconfirmed); "Steamboat Hollow" (no
source). The lock and dam at Bellevue is 1930s too.

### The Ice Jam: three sheets playing as one

- Replaces the river freezing back overnight: a cold night makes skim ice, not sheets that stop a steamboat.
- As we understand it, the river opens from the south. Ice let go upstream drifts down and jams at a narrows, bend, island or bar, the floes riding up and freezing together. To verify: whether the river above Sabula runs narrower between bluffs.
- The three sheets are pieces of one jam, which is why they play as one. Clearing a sheet loosens the rest: the table getting easier is the jam breaking up.
- An ice jam, where the rafters know timber jams: sawmill hands breaking it with axes and pike poles is their everyday work.
- **With the tow:** crews sent out on the ice wear the sheets down before play, like the pushes on the rapids. Not a big deal.
- **Without the tow:** Lars goes out on the ice (no fireman is needed while the boat is stuck), and Willie goes along to learn. Little strength: it takes a very good game.
- **Willie's ducking:** he slips into the river and Lars fishes him out by the collar. A trouble snag (Head Cold or similar): less strength for some days, Willie sniffling under a blanket.
- **Gus cooks** while Willie is laid up. He never complained about the beans; his boiled cabbage is worse, cooked exactly to time. *"Like my mother made it."* The joke is Gus, not Germany.

This is the first table that isn't honest. Everywhere else, everyone's enemy is
everyone else, and you can let them knock each other out. The sheets are collaborating
to defeat you: the same table shape as a tournament, a different game.

**Without the village it's pretty much hopeless.** The lesson is patience, and patience
costs money: a strong ship can afford to wait for the monster hand, so the tow buys the
very thing the table demands. A good, lucky captain can still get through, and that's fine:
they miss the subplots. After a failure the way back to the rafts is open, and Gus points at
it, as a fact: *"Forty men down at the rafts, Captain."*

**The soft play and the whipsaw** are game AI in the old sense: new rules, no language model.
A seat trait with two behaviours:

- **Soft play:** the sheets never fight each other. If you fold, they check it down
  and nothing moves between them.
- **The whipsaw:** when you're in a hand, the two sheets either side of you raise
  back and forth, and every call costs you more.

**The counter is the lesson.** Their raising builds a pot they can only lose to you:
play far fewer hands, wait for a monster, and let them do the betting. It gets easier
as you go: three sheets is brutal, two is a squeeze, and one is plain heads-up. So the
table ends in a thaw.

- **Let the player notice.** No announcement. The sheets just never bet into each
  other, until Gus says it flatly: *"They ain't played a hand against each other all
  morning, Captain."*
- **Names:** a freeze-out is a real poker format and a cold deck a real cheat's term,
  so the table could be the Freeze-Out. Cold shoulders for the puns.
- **Left out:** a beaten sheet merging its pile into its neighbour. Thematic, but it
  punishes the very progress that should feel like a thaw.
- **To build:** that seat trait. Look at how the current AI's betting is built first.

### The tow: a row of ducklings

The *Best Intentions* tows a fleet of rafts up the Mississippi, and for that stretch
she's a village. A kindness that turns out to save you: they pay more than they cost.

**The hardest, slowest, most encumbered days on the river, and perhaps the best days of
their lives.** This is what life can be if you let it. So for this stretch the pressure
comes off the screen: nobody mentions the calendar, and the evenings are the longest and
fullest in the game. It's the only price of the tow: a day or two.

**Fuel: Fill or Half.** At LeClaire, the bunker empty after the climb, it's the Captain's
call: *Fill* ($x) or *Half* ($x/2). Gus: *"Hrmpf. Never leave port without a full bunker."*
Willie is sure there's cheaper fuel at Clinton. It's fair sailing from here, no rapids, so
six loads may not be needed. They're both right: Clinton is a mill town and sells no
coal (keep why vague: a mill town, nobody sells coal), and the cheaper fuel turns out to
be the rafts. Sabula has coal, but dear.

**The rafts are made of fuel.** Built from overbuilt homes on worthless plots, and from
sawmill cut-offs: a cutting board, a chair, a shutter, a door, a windowsill, then the
floorboards. Eventually there's only the soggy foundation left: the house is gone, but it
got them closer to a new life.

**The evening games are the village's own.** The Captain isn't playing the rafters for
their homes. They bring their promises, and the game is how they work out among themselves
who owes what, who gives up the cutting board, the windowsill, the shutter.

- **A regular game; the currency is wood.** Each rafter's stack is their raft. Every pot goes to
  the furnace, whoever wins it, and the game ends when there's enough fuel for tomorrow.
  Winning means keeping more of your home a little longer: resource management, which
  the game was always about.
- **A rotating roster of villagers,** men and women, the cook among them, sits in. Gus, Willie
  and Lars all cede their places at the table on these nights.
- **They can take your snags, and send none back:** it's the one table where snags only
  leave you. (In the engine, close to the `fixes` and `unhands` seat traits.)
- **The tow has to take a few days,** so there are several of these evenings.

- **The row of ducklings dwindles** and the deck of the *Best Intentions* grows more crowded,
  as the furniture, the floorboards and the panelling burn.
- **A real choice:** buy Sabula's dear coal and spare the rafts, or burn homes for free. A
  broke captain burns the floorboards; a flush one doesn't have to. The village arrives with
  whatever you left them. No punishment for burning: it's their gift, and arriving with
  nothing but each other is the poem.
- **Wood firing** is slower, demands more of the firemen, leaves more ash, soots the
  chimney. It's a slower pace and more work, which is the point: more evenings together.
  Fogg burned his steamer's woodwork too, in *Around the World in Eighty Days*.

**What the village brings:**

- **Manpower:** strong sawmill workers. Extra hands, so everyone's rested and the ship's
  strength is at its maximum (they work like hires).
- **The carpenters** refit a storage room as a state room on the move, which allows
  passenger traffic (the boat's `rooms` part). It would cost a fortune at a dock.
- **The cook,** a widow who worked the mansions, seeking work in Dubuque with her two children.
  Everyone's fed, Fatigue goes, Willie's beans get a dignified retirement. Gus: *"Fresh
  biscuits for breakfast. Hm. Could get used to this."*
- **Company:** a full table every evening, rumours running thick. They pay very little.

**The cook's family.** Working names: Mrs. Halloran, Nell and Tam.

- **She offers to stay** with her children, as cook, chambermaid and deckhand. Hiring her isn't
  a stat upgrade: it's trying to keep a little of that life. Only if you took the tow.
- **Her daughter and Willie are sweethearts:** entirely innocent, just sweet. Shy looks, an extra
  biscuit set aside, Willie suddenly combing his hair. No ages on screen.
- **She and Gus:** perhaps. In his way.
- **Who they are** is open: Willie may be a Black lad (see the portraits), and the cook and her
  children could be Black too. We don't have to be historically accurate: it's still 2026,
  even if the art style is 1926.
- **Lars** could get a note from her that nobody else can read.

**The stove and cook move to Dubuque.** They come out of the Tri-Cities' trader (the $15 stove
and $6 cook, in `boat.ts`) and go on sale at Dubuque at a comparable cost, showing what great
value the village's cook is. **Pfund goes:** the name, not the hire. The Dubuque cook could
be just "Cookie".

**Other details:**

- **Why Dubuque:** there isn't much work before it. From memory it had boatyards and
  millwork (to verify), which is enough for "where the work is".
- **The Ice and the village.** Three sheets work together to beat you, and a whole
  village works together for you. Nobody points this out.
- **The swing bridge:** see below.
- **The ending.** The rafts cast off at Dubuque, and the next morning the boat is
  quiet. Gus says so, as a fact. Nobody says "best intentions": the name does that.
- **To simulate:** the Ice Jam with and without the tow, so "pretty much hopeless" is
  true without being impossible.

### The Deadheads: soundings

A snag-heavy stretch, but you get warnings. It teaches choosing your hands with great
care, and looking for the signs and heeding them.

**The Deadheads** is the working name: a real river word for partly sunken logs and
snags, and snags are our dead cards. This is timber lost off the rafts on the way to
Clinton, so the river bed is what's left of the boom. Its pile is soggy lumber.

**Willie sounds the river** from the bow, as cheerful as ever, reading the adversary's
actual hand as it stands on each street. He isn't reading the hand so much as the
danger, and his pole isn't that long: "Mark twain" is the bottom of what he can measure.

| Their hand | Willie calls | Snags | What to do |
|---|---|---|---|
| High card, pair, two pair | "Mark twain!" | Up to 2 (two pair: 1) | Bottom tier. If you can't field a decent two pair, you should have folded |
| Straight, flush, full house | "Mark!" | None | Can cost you money, never snags |
| Three of a kind | "Sawyer!" | 2 | Back out, unless you can beat it |
| Four of a kind | "Planter!" | 1 | Back out |

A sawyer was a sunken tree bobbing in the current, a planter one rooted in the riverbed:
kinds of snag, so "Sawyer!" isn't just a nod to Tom. Trips rank below a straight, so the
scariest call isn't the strongest hand: the player learns to fear the snag, not the rank.

**The adversary** always holds two snags, restocked each hand, and plays as many as its
hand has room for. It's full poker, swaps and rising street fees included, so heeding a
warning early is cheaper.

- **Beat it,** and it forfeits for each snag it risked, on top of the pot: it runs out
  of funds fast. A "Mark twain!" with two snags in it pays you.
- **Lose,** and they land on you.
- **Always fold,** and it pays nothing, as we read the rules. You don't win by folding.
- **Your own snags** go to it if you win: the one stretch where the river takes them
  off you. Another reason to stand.

**Twice.** It comes back on the way down, largely the same, with fewer hands at higher
stakes (down is control). Nobody ever explains "mark twain", and the reach can end on a
long one as you come into safe water.

**To build:** a seat trait that restocks it to two; its snag play made certain (the AI's
hurl fires 35% of the time); forfeits on for this encounter, no upkeep for the river seat,
which is the reverse of other obstacles. Pile, hands and what folding costs: to simulate.

### The dams (background, after our time)

Lock and Dam 13 doesn't mean rough water: the trouble there was too little water,
shallow channels and sandbars, and the dams hold pools to keep nine feet of depth. They
were built for navigation, not power, the exception being the private Keokuk hydro plant
(1910–13), made Lock and Dam 19 in 1930. Congress authorised the nine-foot channel in 1930
"despite a thirty-year decline in upper-river traffic", and it made Depression jobs. In
1904 the job in hand is a six-foot channel, authorised in 1907; from memory that meant wing
dams, already being built (to verify: a possible hazard). The river did not retire: it
lost passengers and general freight and kept bulk cargo. Nearly half of US corn and soybean
exports still go down it by barge.

**Mark Twain:** two fathoms, about twelve feet, the minimum safe depth for a steamboat. Clemens
first used the name in 1863. His own story is that he took it from the pilot Isaiah Sellers, on
thin evidence, and there's a rival story about a bar tab of two drinks.

## The rest of the river

From the drive of 7 November. Drafts, not copy.

### The swing bridge at Sabula

- The span is the meter: play well and it stays open; lose and it swings shut.
- The result is how many of the six rafts get through. Cut-off rafts wait for the next opening and catch up in the morning; nothing is lost but time, and the cut-off rafters miss the evening's game at Sabula.
- The gag: Willie on the whistle cord, a feeble toot, then a blast that knocks the sleeping tender out of his chair. A rafter flattened by the span peels himself off and shakes his fist.
- One animation (the bridge swinging) does the work.

### The Fog Bank

- The community cards (Flop, Turn, River) are hidden under fog. Your own cards stay visible.
- **The whistle** lifts the fog off the whole board for a moment, then it rolls back. It costs strength, which doesn't go in the pot and isn't won back.
- With the village aboard (manpower, raft lumber to burn) you can whistle freely; without it every look costs strength you can't stake.
- **No hand callout in fog games,** not even while the fog is lifted: the player reads and remembers the board. As in real poker. The last encounter before Dubuque, so the training wheels come off at the end of the way up.
- Willie, sniffling: *"Can't see a thing, Captain. You'll have to read it yourself."*

### Dubuque: a hub, the high point

- A hub like the Tri-Cities: repairs, upgrades, hires, trading, a few days.
- The cook and her children may stay on; a raftsman may sign on as fireman.
- Diamond Jo Line, based in Dubuque: a grand steamer with a High Rollers table can be moored there.
- **Pretty Patty comes aboard** over a ramp onto the foredeck (a sternwheeler's freight goes on the main deck, forward; no hold). The inventor drives her up; she stalls halfway; Lars pushes her the rest of the way.
- A victory. An evening of celebration, then the quiet morning after the rafts cast off, then downstream.
- Up is power, down is control: Dubuque is where the shopping list turns to control (rudder, spares, hull). The hubs below are for repair.

### Down: repeats until Muscatine

- Gone: the ice (broken up), the Fog Bank.
- Again: the Deadheads (fewer hands, higher stakes), the rapids, Sandy.

#### The rapids going down

- Ellie's pilot games as before, captains only, no Gaylord.
- Then the descent: the same chains in reverse, heads-up stages. The Long Reach first, the hardest, while the boat is strongest; then Campbell's Chain; then the Moline Chain, the easiest, when strength is lowest. Difficulty falls while strength falls.
- Each stage gets a small, fixed number of hands, high antes from the start. Win decisively and quickly. Run out of hands and you go through anyway, and take a damage snag. It can't stop you, only bruise you.
- Damage snags are removed only by repairs. Their upkeep raises the ante, so every bruise makes the next hand dearer.
- **Losing control:** run out of strength and you can no longer swap or fold. You can only choose which cards to play at the showdown (a snag still goes in if the hand has room). You pick up snags at a higher rate.
- Willie: *"She won't answer the wheel, Captain!"* Gus: *"No turning back now, Captain."* The swap and fold buttons go dark.
- The trap to simulate: spend everything on the Long Reach and the easy Moline Chain wrecks you. Gus: *"It's gonna be a tight squeeze, Captain."*

### The Tri-Cities coming down

- Called the Quad Cities by now: the name took hold while the boat was up north. (The real name only won out around 1930.)
- A dry dock: repairs, odd jobs, a few days.
- **Gaylord's loan** is offered whenever you qualify. Most players will need it here, after the rapids, more than going up.
- Gaylord has outstayed his welcome at Bettendorf (the quads table empties). He waits for the *Best Intentions* and asks for passage; decline and he doesn't come. No departure on a cruiser around day 21.
- **He rides all the way to St. Louis,** for the international High Rollers game at the Fair, and may meet you at the final table. The loan falls due there.
- **At the deck games** he plays for buttons: reads Willie's tells, lectures Lars, wins buttons worth nothing; over the evenings he lets go and laughs. Villain-coded, never a villain. Nobody says he's one of the crew; one evening he's just in Lars's chair.

### Muscatine

- Quiet run down from the Quad Cities.
- **Steamboat Bill:** a visit and a game, news of Patty. He takes troubles off your hands and fixes them on his porch, glad to be useful while he's ashore.
- **Morag and Finlay:** the Finlay subplot may be cut (the story is crowded). Morag keeps the tutorial.

### Below Muscatine

About 275 miles to St. Louis: some 4 or 5 days at roughly 10 mph by day, more with the lock.

| Day | Stretch | Encounter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Muscatine past Burlington | **Sandy again,** just south of Muscatine: the sand washed off going up, relocated and refurbished. *"Welcome back, Captain! Like what we've done with the place?"* Run aground and dig out, the old way. A nostalgic, friendly slog; plenty of strength by now. The last repeat |
| 2+ | to Keokuk | **The lock queue** (may take days) |

No cargo inspector: the federal Steamboat Inspection Service checked boilers, hulls and licences, not freight, and there was no customs between states.

#### The lock queue at Keokuk

- A captains' table game, like the pilot games, but **the lock has capacity**: say three slots. A large packet takes all three, the *Best Intentions* (medium) one, small boats share one. Boats go through in finishing order until the lock is full.
- Not only about winning: if the big packet finishes first it takes the whole lock. Knock it out, or even help a small boat win.
- **Gaylord** can play your seat (he will, as a passenger who wants to get on, but despises you for it, and the warmth at the deck games cools) or be **lent to a small boat** (he's delighted: a captain who understands the game). He can lose: a good human player beats him.
- The lesson of the chapter: play the whole room, not your hand. (The Sandbar: wear it down. The depot: lose on purpose. The rapids up: lose slowly. The rapids down: win fast.)

#### The evening before the Chain

The deck game after the long haul. Everyone tired and a little scared; little said, nobody raises. Lars doesn't sing. Gus: *"Seventeen miles of rock, Captain. Early start."*

#### The Chain of Rocks: the finale

- About 17 miles of limestone ledges beginning just north of St. Louis, below the mouth of the Missouri. Impassable at low water, dangerous at high water; bypassed by the Chain of Rocks Canal in 1953. No named ledges found in the sources.
- Not the Grand Chain (near Thebes, below St. Louis), which Twain wrote about.
- **No pilot.** The Captain takes it; at most a pilot for hire at Alton, a purchase, no game.
- A chained event that draws on earlier lessons without throwing in everything. Stages hung on real landmarks:
  - **The Big Muddy** (the Missouri's nickname): the river's mouth as a greedy, bottomless mouth. It swallows everything put in the pot and can't be busted. Played for a fixed number of hands, drifting past it; what counts is how much is left of you. Its mud clouds the water (the board half hidden, as in the fog).
  - **Chouteau Island**, along which the ledges run (after the Chouteau family, fur traders who helped found St. Louis in 1764).
  - **The Eads Bridge** (1874) at St. Louis as the finish line.
- Then the Fair, and the international High Rollers table.
- Number of stages and their mechanics: open.

### Why the river, not the railroad

- Roads were mostly dirt, mud in April; the AAA tour that summer set out to prove cars could survive them at all.
- Rail was the sensible way: four railroads ran through Dubuque by 1900, among them the Burlington Route, which also reached St. Louis. A day or two by freight car. The 1904 rate for a crated car: not found.
- The story's reason: **Steamboat Bill owes the inventor a favour,** so the river is free. It also explains why Bill got the job and why it pays so little.
- Other options: the inventor's showmanship (arrive by steamboat at the levee and drive off under her own power), or not trusting the railroads with his only machine.

### The ending

- Fanfare at St. Louis: Patty drives off the gangplank under her own power, the band, confetti, the crowd following her up the levee.
- Then the levee empty, the deck game one last time. Gus: *"There's freight for Memphis in the morning, Captain."* The crew belong on the river.
- **The League is the Fair's High Rollers table,** separate from the story, for players who want more.
- What the story is about: family, helping out, making the best of it. Patty is the reason to set off.

### Releasing the story

Chapter by chapter, like a film serial. Chapter one: Morag's lessons, Muscatine, Sandy, the Tri-Cities, ending at the foot of the rapids with an intertitle: *Next time: the Rock Island Rapids!*

### The Big Muddy

- The Missouri personified: a huge, hungry mouth drooling mud. Not supernatural: real hazard, cartoon face, like Sandy.
- **A bottomless purse:** you can't outbid it, outlast it or bust it. Endless appetite, endless capacity. You only get past it.
- Background only (never on screen): Fenrir bound with Gleipnir, a fetter that included the roots of a mountain, and the river Ván running from his jaws, in Snorri.
- Draft stages: 1) the Big Muddy, a fixed number of hands, the board half hidden by mud; 2) the ledges past Chouteau Island, win decisively or take strikes, losing control if strength runs out; 3) the run to the Eads Bridge, arriving with what's left.
- **The lesson of the Big Muddy: don't feed it.** Everything put in is swallowed, so stay small and quiet, fold cheaply, bet only when nearly certain. Sneak past; never try to empty it.
- **The ledges, proposed:** a chain of seven links, each link one single hand. Win and you slide over the ledge; lose and you scrape it, a strike and a damage snag. No rest between. Gus counts them off: *"Four more, Captain."* Asks for the Deadheads' reading and the rapids' decisiveness at once.
- **The Eads Bridge, proposed:** as far as we know, steamboat men complained it was too low for tall chimneys, and some boats had hinged chimneys (to verify). Lowering the stacks costs draught and steam: a last hand played with reduced strength. Not a hand you must lose: win it and you sail in proudly, lose it and you limp in under the bridge. Either way you arrive.
- **Settled shape of the finale:** 1) the Big Muddy; 2) the seven links; 3) the arrival under the Eads Bridge as a vignette, not a game (after the links, another hand would be padding). Willie and Gus crank the chimneys down, smoke everywhere, the boat ducks under, the Fair opens up beyond.
- **Seven links, rules:** the rocks send a snag with any hand they win, even a straight or full house (an exception to the normal rule). Folding is allowed: reversing the wheel against the current, it costs strength, you don't advance, the same link waits. At zero strength, control is lost and folding goes (the existing rule).
- **Front-loaded:** win the first links and the current can carry you over the last ones. Snags raise the ante and drain strength faster, so it snowballs: simulate so it doesn't spiral too hard. You always arrive; the question is how battered.
- **Ending variety:** a careful captain arrives clean; a reckless one limps in listing and smoking, a paddle missing, Patty spotless on the foredeck.
- Also on the shelf: the Chain of Rocks as a necklace on a grand lady dressed for the Fair.

## A day on the river

Taking on cargo, passengers and crew landing by landing makes the voyage a
small sim, and gives the days a rhythm, one landing a day:

1. **Morning, at the moorings.** Take on cargo and passengers, hire a second
   fireman if the stretch ahead is hard, buy coal (at the landing's price),
   cast off. The sim part: what you carry, who rides, what it costs.
2. **On the river.** Something happens: a sandbar, a fog bank, a runaway mule,
   a boat in trouble, a passenger's crisis (the river's snags, met as events).
3. **Evening, the landing.** Make landing, moor, disembark; supper as darkness
   falls.
4. **Night, the game.** Wherever there's one to be had: on deck with the
   passengers, with other travellers or the landing's regulars; at the
   saloon, if the town has one; or aboard a **grand steamer**, if one is
   moored there that night. The night's game is the day's encounter.

**Which way people travel.** The first leg goes up, away from St. Louis: the
passengers aboard are going *up* (to Dubuque and the towns between), never
to the Fair. Anyone bound for St. Louis is met on the way up and wants
passage on the way back down: a booking made with a handshake, one more
obligation to remember when the boat comes past again.

## The river in segments

The river comes in stretches, each between two gates. Inside a stretch you
can go up and down as you please: to deliver a job, settle a snag, or try a
table again. The gates are where the river itself says no: you don't go back
down past the Rock Island Rapids until you've done what needs doing above
them, because of the pilot and the cost of climbing them again (it's hard
going, and the coal shoveller earns his keep that day). Almost open world,
tightly scoped and bite-sized: a few planned encounters and characters per
stretch, a few jobs and snags to resolve, and a gate to pass.

1. **Muscatine to the Tri-Cities.** Muscatine, the Tri-Cities, the coal
   yard. Visited going up and again coming down.
   *Gate:* **the Rock Island Rapids**: Ellie Sawyer's table, and coal enough
   to climb.
2. **Above the rapids: LeClaire to Dubuque.** Patty and the advance on her.
   More to find (Clinton, Bellevue: to look into).
   *Gate:* the rapids again, going down: Ellie plays for that too.
3. **Below Muscatine: Burlington to Keokuk.**
   *Gate:* **the Des Moines Rapids**: the canal at Keokuk, the queue at the
   lock.
4. **Keokuk to St. Louis.** Hannibal, Alton, St. Louis.

## Money is the fuel

Poker is the engine of the river; money is its fuel. The young captain is
underfunded: the jobs don't pay well enough to keep the boat going, so every
stretch costs more than the work brings in, and the difference is won at the
table. **One purse all the way down** (the captain's; the crew's strength is
another thing, below).

- **Every leg has a price.** Coal, mostly: a dock will always sell it, at a
  premium. Also pilots, lock fees, harbour dues. To go on, you need the sum;
  the encounter that unlocks the next stretch has a stake you must afford.
- **Where the money comes from:** Bill's starting cash in Muscatine; the
  **advance on Pretty Patty** in Dubuque; and until then, winnings, snag
  resolutions, odd jobs and small freight.
- **Side jobs:** play someone for the privilege of a well-paying job (a load
  that goes further up, or back down to settle a snag), and get paid enough
  for the next ante.
- **Never stuck.** Losing costs time, not the game: there's always an odd job
  at poor pay (carrying something, working aboard someone else's boat), so a
  thin purse is a detour, never a dead end.

### Two purses: money and strength

- **The captain's purse (coins).** Fares, freight, jobs, and winnings at the
  landing tables, against strangers and rivals. It pays for coal, supplies,
  upgrades and wages.
- **The crew's strength (buttons).** Made fresh each morning, a few buttons
  from each hand aboard (Morag's bone buttons, already in the game). It's
  what the boat throws against an obstacle (the stake against the Sandbar is
  effort, not dollars), and what's left by evening is what the crew bring to
  the deck game. After a hard day: a few buttons, nothing left to give.

The captain never plays his crew for their wages: they pool their effort.
The two meet through the boat:

- **Purse into strength:** food, hammocks, a better galley stove raise each
  morning's strength. The crew belong to the boat; the captain pays their
  wages and feeds and lodges them, so their strength is the boat's.
- **Strength into purse:** odd jobs at a landing, the crew's work for pay.
- **Coal stays in the purse:** the cost of each stretch, bought in the
  morning; without enough of it you don't cast off.

### Trade: north and south of the rapids

The rapids split the river's economy, and the winter has cut the upper river
off, so whoever comes through first in spring can sell at a premium. Any boat
can pay cash for goods at one landing and sell them at another, a few sacks
or crates at a time, on deck space shared with cargo and passengers.

- **Bought below, sold above:** cotton, bourbon, corn, molasses, sugar,
  tobacco: dear up north after a long winter.
- **Bought above, sold below:** woven cloth, flour and grain, beer, nails,
  machine parts, lumber: cheap at the source.
- **Muscatine's own:** pearl buttons.
- **The catch: it binds up cash.** Money in crates can't pay for coal, a
  pilot or a seat at a table. A hold full of bourbon and an empty purse at
  the foot of the rapids is a very good story.
- **Carried freight is different:** a job pays you to carry someone else's
  goods, risks nothing and pays less. Trade is your own money on your own
  judgment.

Each landing's prices are a table, like the trader's goods; a stretch's
prices fall as more boats get through, so being early pays. (By 1904 the
railroads carried most of this; the fable lets the river keep it. The
goods are from general knowledge, to verify as landings are written.)

### The deck games

The crew's after-supper game is communal, not competitive, and it should feel
different from the landing tables:

- **For buttons, never coins,** which honours Morag's rule (*"You should
  NEVER play for money!"*): she'd approve. Buttons won and lost stay in the
  crew; the pool fills again overnight.
- **What winning does:** gets the right trouble into the right hands (the
  boiler to the engineer, see Where snags go), and a good evening might lend
  tomorrow a button or two.
- **The feel:** lantern light after supper, Lars singing, coffee not whiskey,
  warm lines, no "you lose" verdict. When the buttons run out the game simply
  ends, and each evening closes on a line of its own, in turn: the wick
  gutters, the moon is out as Lars sings, a frog complains about the weather.

### The morning council and the trader

Each day brings its own challenge, and the wise captain confers with his
crew before buying anything or hiring anyone. The obstacles are fixed, and
the crew know the river: *"Fog's thick below Bellevue this time of year"*
(Willie); *"The rapids'll want a second shovel, Captain"* (Lars); *"She'll burn
half again going up"* (Gus). Each suggests what the day needs; the captain
decides. A tutorial in all but name, and a voice for every hand aboard.

**A trader at each landing,** with what the place has (cheap lumber at
Clinton, buttons at Muscatine) and what the day ahead calls for. Buying takes
money out of the gambling purse, but makes the boat as a whole more
productive: more strength each morning, which is both what the crew throw at
obstacles and what they bring to the deck games. Hiring is the same counter:
**a pilot** for the rapids (required), **a second fireman** going up (wise).

Upgrades and supplies, first drafts:

| Item | Does |
|---|---|
| Repair materials | Fix a trouble without waiting for its fixer |
| Food for the crew (better than beans) | More strength tomorrow |
| Hammocks | More strength every morning |
| A better galley stove | More strength every morning; and a cook might sign on |
| An upgraded passenger cabin, furniture | Better fares, better passengers |
| Spare pole, spare paddle | A trouble you never get |
| **The whistle** | Can save your life in the fog bank (say: a free hand there, or the fog's pile cut short), and a place in the queue at the lock |

### Stations

Everything aboard is rated on the same three tiers, 0, 1 or 2. The vessel has
boiler, engine, kitchen, rooms (Storage, Cabin, Luxury), rudder and paddles; each
hand has a rating at each station, and stands at one. The Captain has the bridge.

- **Furnace** (boiler) and **Engine room** (engine): asset × rating × 3 buttons.
- **Galley** (kitchen): asset × the best cook's rating, for everyone else.
- **Deck** (rooms and hold): nothing yet; passengers and cargo, when they come.
- Every hand makes 2 buttons just being aboard.

So Lars at the furnace and Gus in the engine room make 18 with Willie, and a better
engine doubles Gus's work. It's fully automatic: everyone starts in the right
place, and moving them is only for those who want it, in the ship menu. **The
ship menu**, behind the turning paddle wheel, shows the bunker, ship's strength,
who stands where, hires, repairs, the vessel. A hire lasts the day they sign on
and the next, then renews for their wage if re-hire is ticked (the default) and
you can pay. At the deck game a hire takes Lars's chair, a second takes Willie's.

**Backlog: snag counters.** A spare aboard meets a specific snag: Sandy sends a
Broken Pole, Willie says *"No worries! We have a spare!"*, the spare is spent and
the snag is swatted back. Insurance as a boat upgrade.

### Stats: the boat and the crew

Every purchase and hire changes a number, so both need a few, kept as data
(one table each, like the snags):

- **The Best Intentions:** coal bunker (how much she carries), speed up and
  down, berths (how many passengers), deck space (how much cargo: a full hold
  is a full hand of snags), comfort (galley, hammocks: the morning strength
  bonus), equipment (whistle, spares, lanterns).
- **Each crew member:** strength per day (buttons made each morning), what
  they fix (the engineer the boiler, Lars the pole, Willie fatigue and broken
  hearts), wage, appetite (Lars eats for two), and their play style at the
  deck game. Temporary hands (a second fireman, a pilot) have the same, plus
  a day rate.

## The grand steamers

The big passenger and excursion steamers moor for the night somewhere new
each evening, at a landing or out on the water, like cruise ships; smaller
boats tie up alongside, and the evening is cards, supper and whiskey aboard
the grandest of them. That's where the **High Rollers tables** are, and since
the steamers move, those tables turn up anywhere along the river.

- **A small fleet**, met up and down the river, each with its own house,
  table and regulars (first drafts):
  - the ***Lucrezia***, the most opulent, Italianate and gilded; Eliza
    "Bone Pale" Bathory's favourite;
  - the ***Persephone***, which spends half the year on the upper river and
    half below (the underworld, by the upper river's reckoning);
  - the ***Gilded Age*** (after Twain and Warner's novel of 1873), all new
    money and electric light;
  - one more to find: an excursion boat, cheaper and louder, dancing on the
    upper deck.
  Every one of them has a **High Rollers table**. Some saloons have one too
  (the Tri-Cities' best, Dubuque's).
- **Dress is the invitation.** Win a decent suit and waistcoat (at a table,
  or as a job's payment), and you're asked aboard. Wardrobe as progression.
- **Real flavour** (from memory, to verify): the **Diamond Jo Line**, run out
  of Dubuque, carried passengers between St. Louis and St. Paul until about
  1911; the **Streckfus** excursion steamers worked out of Rock Island. The
  riverboat gambler's heyday was before the Civil War, so by 1904 it's half
  legend, which suits a 1927 cartoon that romanticises it anyway.

## Who is where

- **Muscatine: Finlay**, "Carouser", carouses here and hereabouts, and
  **Morag** hunts him down. Passing Muscatine a few times (up, and down
  again) lets their story unfold in instalments: where he was seen, what he
  owes, how close she came. Whether they ever meet is open: their showdown
  may stay unresolved, they may be background characters sailing past each
  other on the river, or a reconciliation may turn up as the story grows.
- **Gaylord Montague, "Esquire"**, of Bettendorf (where else would a
  card-sharp live than a town that bets on anything?), but met at LeClaire's
  saloon. Going up he offers **the loan** whatever the night's outcome: he
  has a stash (he plays best when the odds are against him, so rarely spikes
  a pot), and he likes the young captain's style: *"Fearless and audacious.
  I see myself in you."* If he finishes first or second it's sweeter still:
  he's on a lucky streak, and he knows himself well enough to invest it
  before he squanders it (Gaylord's sponsorship, below).
  Coming back down he snitches on Finlay to Morag and wants **passage** until
  he can board a grand steamer, and plays for it: if he loses he pays the
  pot, if he wins he rides for free. At Hannibal the grand steamer comes,
  and he'll need the loan back before he goes aboard. He travels: he can turn
  up at any High Rollers table.
- **LeClaire: Ellie Sawyer**, the rapids pilot: her High Rollers table going
  up, and again coming down.
- **Anywhere a grand steamer moors: Eliza Bathory, "Bone Pale"**, no
  captain: a high roller at the grand steamers' tables, now and then. She plays for sport
  and has money to spare: she sets aside a sum to lose each night, and
  usually retires with the pot.

## The crew of the Best Intentions

A small sternwheel freighter, and she runs tight. By law (the federal
steamboat inspectors; from memory, to verify) a steamboat carried a licensed
master, pilot and engineer; on a boat this small the master is often the
pilot too, but the engineer is always his own man. Everyone else doubles up.
Names and personalities below are first drafts.

- **The captain: the player.** Unnamed, young, and nobody else aboard: not Willie, but Bill's First Mate, made captain in the first scene, and the clerk as
  well: the books, the purse, the freight bills are all his. No pilot's
  licence and no time to get one; the cartoon doesn't fuss, except where the
  river does: the rapids want a real pilot (Ellie).
- **Willie, deck hand and galley boy.** The narrator and the player's helper
  (roster.md): tips, captions, his reckoning on each captain. Lines, loading,
  and the crew's supper, which is mostly beans.
- **Steamboat Bill, "Captain Bill".** The old captain, ashore: his wife has taken ill, and there are little ones to care for. May turn
  up again down the river.
- **The engineer: Augustus "Gus" Klemm.** From Davenport's Germans (a large
  community then). Meticulous, oil to the elbows, talks to the engine (who has
  a name of her own, *Old Hildegard* (a placeholder), and moods). Knows to the shovel what
  each stretch will burn: the voice of coal and the cost of going up. A steam
  man with a gasoline motor crated on deck: he scorns Pretty Patty in public
  and sneaks out at night to look at her.
- **The fireman: Lars Brekke.** Norwegian, enormous, nearly silent; eats
  enough for two and shovels enough for three. He speaks in musical notes:
  Scandinavian is sing-song to everyone aboard, and nobody understands him
  anyway. After a week or so he has a few simple words (*"Sjekk"*,
  *"Fåld"*).
- **The second fireman, hired by the stretch.** For the hard going (up the
  rapids, against a spring flood), the captain takes on a second fireman at
  the landing: an expense, priced by the stretch. Some of them are the
  **Unionized Coal Shoveller**, the snag that already exists: he works to
  rule, breaks on the hour, and is a pleasure to pass on.

**A cook?** Not for four. With passengers aboard there are mouths enough,
and that can be a passenger's fare: someone who pays her passage in the
galley (below).

## Passengers

Rotating travellers, each with somewhere to be: they come aboard at one
landing and leave at another, and while aboard they're company at the rail,
a voice at supper, now and then an opponent. Passage is money, and the
boat needs money; some passengers are better company than fares, and some
are **snags** (the In-Laws, the Layabout Nephew and the Travelling Preacher
already are). Going up, the passengers are bound up the river; most of
these are bound for St. Louis, so they're met on the way up and ride on the
way back down. First drafts:

- **Mrs. Hannelore Pfund**, a widow going down to her daughter in St. Louis.
  Once cooked on a packet boat; pays her fare in the galley, in pies and in
  opinions (on the beans, on poker, on Willie's posture). Morag's natural
  ally, or rival.
- **Ambrose Quill**, a drummer (a travelling salesman) with two sample cases,
  getting off at every other landing. Sells anything, plays poker eagerly
  and badly, and pays his debts in samples (a snag waiting to happen).
- **A clammer from Muscatine** with a pearl in his pocket. The pearl-button
  works paid for shells, but now and then a mussel held a freshwater pearl,
  and the upper Mississippi had a pearl rush round the turn of the century
  (from memory, to verify). He's taking his to sell in St. Louis and trusts
  nobody, least of all card players.
- **A young photographer with a Kodak Brownie** (new in 1900), bound for the
  Fair to photograph everything. Every landing ends in a group portrait
  (a frozen frame in the film); she wants a picture of Pretty Patty more than
  anything.
- **The Reverend**, going to the Fair to save souls on **the Pike** (its
  amusement street). Disapproves of the card table loudly, and asks
  quietly, later, how the betting works. The Travelling Preacher snag, met
  as a person first.
- **Honeymooners** going to the Fair, who argue about everything and are
  delighted with each other.
- **Morag**, chasing Finlay; **Gaylord**, coming down, if he wins his
  passage.

## A river of all sorts

The river in 1904 was anything but all white: Black rivermen worked every
boat, St. Louis had a large Black community and was the capital of ragtime,
the upper river was Sauk, Meskwaki and Ho-Chunk country within living
memory, and the towns were full of Germans, Irish, Scandinavians and
Bohemians. A cast with nobody but white faces would be its own distortion.
The trap runs both ways: put the only Black character on the coal pile and
it's the old caricature; leave everyone out to be safe and the river is
empty. Cartoons of the 1920s leaned hard on racial caricature (rivermen and
cooks among their targets); we take their drawing, music and timing, and
leave that out.

**Principles:**

- **Range, not a token.** Every group turns up more than once, across
  stations: captains and passengers, rich and broke, sharps and fools, crew
  and gentry. A Black cook is fine when she isn't the only Black person on
  the river.
- **The joke is the personality, never the origin.** Everyone is a
  caricature here, of a temperament (Willie's eagerness, Morag's rectitude,
  Gaylord's manners), not of a people.
- **Accents for all or none.** Morag's Scots, Lars's Norwegian, the
  engineer's German (or Scots): the captions give everyone their voice, and
  nobody's is the funny one.
- **Everybody wants something.** Their own goal, their own wit, the last
  word now and then. Nobody exists to serve someone else's scene.
- **Mind the drawing.** Rubber-hose cartoons grew partly out of minstrelsy
  (the white gloves, the wide mouths). The portraits draw everyone as a
  person with their own features, in the same style and with the same care.

**People to draw from** (first drafts):

- **The pianist on the grand steamers.** St. Louis was ragtime's capital
  (Scott Joplin wrote *The Cascades* for the 1904 Fair; from memory, to
  verify). A Black pianist who plays the High Rollers tables, knows every
  regular's tells, and is, in a silent film, the one supplying the music: the
  score is his. Sharp, unhurried, in on every joke.
- **A St. Louis businesswoman** heading home from a selling trip upriver: hair
  preparations, sold door to door and through agents (the flavour of Annie
  Turnbo Malone, whose Poro company began in St. Louis around 1902; fictional,
  not her). Shrewd, well dressed, a formidable card player: a match for
  Eliza at the grand tables.
- **Mrs. Pfund, or whoever pays her fare in the galley,** could be Black once
  the river has these others: her pride (a packet-boat cook, and the best on
  the river) and her opinions are what she's made of.
- **The second fireman,** hired by the stretch, is a different man each time:
  Irish, Black, Bohemian, a farm boy. Rotating, so no group is "the
  shoveller".
- **A Meskwaki passenger** going to the Fair, where its anthropology exhibits
  put living people on show (Geronimo among them; from memory). He's been
  asked to appear and is going to negotiate his fee: wry, practical, and
  better informed about the river than anyone aboard. No feathers, no
  mysticism. The Meskwaki had bought their own land at Tama, Iowa, in 1857
  (to verify).
- **The river's mixed families.** Antoine LeClaire, after whom LeClaire is
  named, was of French and Potawatomi descent (from memory). The rapids
  pilots could come from such families, Ellie Sawyer among them, if it suits
  her.
- **Others to place:** Bohemian Czechs (Iowa had them; Dvořák summered in
  Spillville in 1893), Irish, a Chinese American (not a laundry), Jewish
  merchants (never as the money-lender or the sharp).
- **Already in the gallery: Lenny "Troubadour" King**, a wandering
  troubadour with a cardboard violin (no captain, no boat), Jewish, and
  doesn't make a thing of it. His gloom is his own, not his people's.
- **The engineer: German or Scots?** A Scots engineer is a fine tradition:
  Kipling's *McAndrew's Hymn* (1894, free), the old engineer praying to his
  engines, and later Scotty. But Morag and Finlay are already Scots; a third
  could be the joke (Morag and the engineer arguing in a dialect nobody else
  aboard can follow), or one too many.

**A cartoonist worth remembering:** George Herriman, who drew *Krazy Kat*
(Ignatz is in the gallery), was a Creole of colour from New Orleans,
working in the very style we borrow.

**Settled in the roster:** James Robert "Jim-Bob" Lee read like an echo of
Robert E. Lee (also a famous steamboat, of the 1870 race against the
*Natchez*), and had little else going for him: retired.

## Characters with secrets

- **Max Moritz, Captain of the Mischief.** At the table, one very tall captain
  in a very long coat (the only hint: *"The coat giggles."*). Story Mode
  reveals the truth: Wilhelm Busch's two rascals, Max and Moritz, one standing
  on the other's shoulders. Once seated, one of them slips out under the table
  to swap cards and peek at hands. Their play style (the "griefer": not out to
  win, out to make you lose) fits; the reveal could come with a new rule for
  that match (they can see one of your cards, say), or simply as the joke.
- They **take turns on top.** Between rounds the coat changes hands: the
  portrait shows whichever boy is wearing the top half this round, and the
  play style follows him (one cautious, one reckless, say), while the other is
  off doing mischief under the table. One captain on the table, two
  personalities to read: attentive players can learn to tell them apart.

- **Sandy keeps bar.** The Sandbar presents as a barman (*"Today's specials
  are the Broken Paddle and the Twisted Axle"*) and is always chipper: wash
  him away and he only drifts off somewhere else. A quip every other hand, in
  asides.

## Sandy's routine

An obstacle's lines are a **routine** (`src/story/mechanics/routine.ts`), sorted by the job
they do, so each kind can be judged on its own. A joke is excellent, or it's out: silence
beats filler. The same shape will serve the Rapids' stages, the Deadheads and the Fog Bank.

- **Opening:** the first time you meet. One line.
- **Mornings:** each day after, in order; **the last one repeats.** Until there's more than
  one good joke, the first is the last.
- **Rounds:** before a round now and then (Sandy: the third hand), in rotation, answered on
  the buttons. An answer may get a comeback, or simply deal. Trivia works here: it doesn't
  matter what the Captain answers.
- **One-liners:** a dozen, exactly, at random, never twice in a day; once they're spent,
  he says nothing more that day. They come when a hand goes his way (you lost it, or folded):
  a small reward when it doesn't go yours.
- **Last call:** at the start of the day's last round. **Cleared** and **beaten:** how it ends.

Sandy's, in `src/story/characters/sandy.ts`:

- **Opening:** "Welcome to the sandbar, I'll be your waiter this evening. Today's specials are
  the Broken Paddle and the Twisted Axle."
- **Morning:** "Welcome back, Captain, have a seat. It's five o'clock somewhere, I'm sure."
- **Rounds:** "Another round, Captain?" (Leave: "You wish." Deal: "Shore thing.") "Pub quiz,
  Captain! Name a structure for retaining water." (Well, Dam: and deal.)
- **The dozen** (a slash is a two-liner: the setup, a beat, the punchline):
  1. A deuce drifts into a sandbar, orders a beer. / Says: "Put it on my bill."
  2. It wasn't easy for me, growing up. / You see, my mother was a beach.
  3. You are a very grounded person, Captain.
  4. What's your poison, Captain? We've got silt, and we've got grit. / Moonshine too, if you
     stick around.
  5. This is a very fine sediment, Captain.
  6. The nerve of some people! / Calling me shallow!
  7. I aspire to be a Beach Club one day.
  8. ♪ Sand and deliver! ♪ / ♪ Your money and your time! ♪ (after Adam Ant)
  9. I used to be a mountain, you know. / Erosion was a constant grind.
  10. I hope you like the impression I made of your hull. / But wait until you see my work on
      the engine.
  11. Do you know what the best thing about sand is? / Sand gets everywhere. (Anakin hated it
      for exactly that.)
  12. ♪ Do you like Piña Coladas? ♪ / ♪ And getting stuck on the river? ♪ (after Rupert Holmes)
- **Last call!** **Cleared:** "I'll drift off downstream now… Catch you later, Captain!"
  **Beaten:** "I think you've had enough, Captain. You're in no shape to drive: you'd better
  stay the night."

**The workshop:** something in them, not there yet.
