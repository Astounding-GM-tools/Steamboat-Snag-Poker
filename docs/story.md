# Story Mode: design notes

Working title: **Steamboat to St. Louis.**

Status: **the tutorial is built and live** (Morag's lessons, see
[tutorial.md](tutorial.md)), and with it the caption system: speakers,
placement, beats, replies, moves asked for, show-me taps, outcomes and
snags. The table takes any number of captains, and a story can say who sits
at yours, landing by landing, with their decks and moves scripted. The story
itself is still a proposal; its lines are drafts to argue over, not copy.

## The premise

**Every encounter ends in a game of Snag Poker.** It is silly, and it is a
proud tradition: in a fighting game every meeting ends in a fight, in an
action game the story sets up the shooting, the sneaking or the race, a
superhero film sets up its set pieces, and Might and Magic: Clash of Heroes
has characters and story galore that always end in a match-three puzzle. On
our river, Snag Poker is simply how things are settled: by harbour masters,
miners, inspectors and rival captains alike. Camp, played straight.

**A 1927 cartoon about the spring of 1904.** The look is the mid-1920s; the
story is set in April 1904. That's no contradiction: it's a cartoon made in
1927 looking back on the good old days of steamboats and straw boaters, the
way the films of the 1920s looked back on the Gay Nineties. Its voice is fond,
a little knowing, and free with the facts.

**The deadline is the World's Fair.** The Louisiana Purchase Exposition in
St. Louis opened on 30 April 1904 (to verify), and automobiles were on show
there: the horseless carriage has to be there for opening day. Gentle
pressure, never a threat.

**No villain, no conflict to speak of.** This is not the Odyssey. It's about
fulfilling obligations: getting the cargo to where the boss has been paid to
deliver it, and every other job taken on along the way. Nobody is chasing the
boat, nobody hires card sharps to stop it (Henry Ford is not sabotaging
anything), and the captains you beat bear no grudge: everyone you meet would
rather like to win the hand, and is a good sport when they don't. It isn't even
the end of the world if you never reach St. Louis.

**Touchstones:** *Three Men in a Boat* (1889), a comic river journey of
episodes, digressions and eccentrics, without a villain or real stakes;
*Around the World in 80 Days* for the delivery and the deadline (minus its
detective); *Kiki's Delivery Service* for the young courier making their way
among strangers, a job at a time, kindness and a dip in confidence instead of
a villain; *Paddington* (the 2014 film) for the newcomer and a cast of
eccentrics won over, but drier, not so twee; *Silver Linings Playbook* for
messy, likeable people with troubles of their own, a family that bickers
and means well, a wager riding on the outcome, and a contest at the end
where the score that counts is a modest one, not first place.
Huckleberry Finn has the river, but more danger than we want.
A cozy road movie on water: the tension is small, practical and comic.
Obligations pile up (snags as cargo, jobs taken on with a handshake), things
go off (the eggs, the ice), there's a queue at the lock.

**What follows for the game:**

- **Losing is never punishing.** Lose an encounter and you pay something (a
  sum, a detour, an extra snag, a day) and the voyage goes on. No game over on
  the river; not reaching St. Louis is just a different, sillier ending.
- **Opponents are acquaintances, not enemies.** They come back, remember you
  fondly, want a rematch, owe you or you owe them.
- **Each landing is a short reel:** a vignette with its own set-up, its own
  little twist on the game, and a punchline as you leave. Written one at a
  time, together, the way the hands lesson was.

## Principles

- **Captions are a literary form.** Every line is written, read aloud, and
  turned over before it goes in. No filler, no generated padding. Fewer, better
  lines beat many average ones.
- **Short and punchy,** like the silent films: one sentence, two at most,
  plain words, a period flavour, a joke when one is earned.
- **Never in the way.** Every caption can be dismissed; none blocks a decision
  for longer than it takes to read.
- **The game teaches; the story motivates.** Rules come through play, not
  through text walls.
- **No feature creep.** A mechanic goes in when an encounter we actually want
  needs it, not before.

## The story

**Muscatine.** The old captain, **Steamboat Bill** (everyone aboard calls him
Father; the player is no relation, just his trusted deckhand), has just landed a Big Snag: deliver a horseless carriage to the World's
Fair in St. Louis. That is how fin-de-siècle logistics work. The carriage
waits upriver in Dubuque: a short run up to fetch it, and then it is all
downriver, an easy passage.

Then family calls him ashore (someone to tend to; between cargo and family,
family wins). The deckhand, the player, is made captain: *"You're the captain
now. Just stay away from those whiskey-swilling river gamblers and their Snag
Poker!"*

Except the passage isn't easy. There are snags everywhere. The harbour master
won't clear the boat unless you beat him at Snag; the captains ahead in the
queue at the bridge works will trade their place for a hand; the mail bag goes
with whoever wins it. Coal moves the boat, but Snag Poker moves the river.

So the young captain breaks the promise to Father at the first landing, and
Morag's ("You should NEVER play for money!") soon after, and keeps breaking
them all the way to St. Louis.

**No villains.** Nobody is chasing the boat, nobody wants it sunk. It is
river life, and poker is a large part of it. Every encounter is a transaction:
passage, funds, a place in the queue, a job taken on or handed off.

**The goal: honour your obligations.** Reach the Fair with the carriage, and
every other job you took on along the way delivered or passed on fair and
square. Then the Fair's great river tournament opens, and the League
begins.

## The cargo: Pretty Patty

The horseless carriage is a prototype, the only one of its kind, crated on
deck. Its makers call it something grand and patented; the crew call it
**Pretty Patty**. The irony stays unstated: the boat is carrying the
instrument of its own undoing. In a few decades small wheels on tarmac will
take over from the river, and the ambling life of landings and steamboats
will go with them. Nobody aboard suspects; the cartoon of 1927 knows, and
says nothing.

**Its maker is fictional, its flavour real.** A river-town workshop, an
eccentric inventor, one machine built, and a reputation riding on it reaching
the Palace of Transportation. Period cars went by the maker's name and the
word "patent"; the full name is still to settle (Pettibone's Patent Motor
Buggy was one idea). Real background to borrow from:

- **Adams-Farwell of Dubuque, Iowa**, upriver from Muscatine, made cars from
  1903, with a rotary engine whose cylinders spun round a crankshaft standing
  still. A cartoon engine: it whirls of its own accord. (Whether they showed
  at the Fair: to verify.)
- **The rival delivery.** In the summer of 1904 the AAA ran a tour of
  motorcars from New York, Philadelphia and Baltimore to the Fair, about 1,350
  miles in 18 days, to prove cars could manage America's roads; it began the
  Glidden Tours. Dozens of cars driving in overland, and one floating down
  the river.
- **The Palace of Transportation** was the Fair's second-largest exhibit
  hall: Benz, Daimler and Panhard & Levassor showed cars there, beside
  locomotives.

**A Fair a year late.** The Louisiana Purchase was made in 1803: Napoleon,
his Caribbean empire lost with the failed expedition to Saint-Domingue and
war with Britain about to resume, sold the territory for $15 million. The
centennial Fair was meant for 1903 and was put off to 1904, to let more
states and nations take part, and because the building ran late. A story
about racing to reach it on time can let that lie, or wink at it once.

## Snags as cargo

In the League a snag is a penalty to pass on. In the story it is cargo and
duty: the mail bag, the stinking hides, the eggs that must not go rotten, the
horseless carriage itself. It works the same at the table, and reads
differently:

- **Upkeep is the cost of carrying it:** feed for the livestock, ice for the
  eggs.
- **Pawning it** is another captain taking the job off your hands, which is
  the mechanic we already have.
- **Delivering it** is new: at the right landing (or simply "in St. Louis"),
  the snag leaves your hand and the obligation is met.
- **A full hand of snags is a full hold,** and a reason to take a detour or
  play someone for their place.
- Some snags are **reputation**, not cargo: decline the captain's code and you
  are handed *Yellow-belly* for free. It goes when you pass it on, like any
  snag.

## Encounters

Every meeting, the tutorial included, is a match with its own settings plus a
script:

| Setting | Options |
|---|---|
| Who | One captain, or a table of several |
| Pot | Off, or on |
| Currency | Coins, or buttons (the tutorial) |
| Deck | Shuffled, or arranged hand by hand |
| Format | Until someone's broke, a set number of hands, best of three |
| Stakes | The ante, plus whatever a reply adds ("I'll put $5 in the pot") |
| Script | The conversation and its triggers |

Story mode is a chain of encounters, and flags decide which lines play.

**An encounter must be solved to go on.** Until it is, it is the only thing
to do: no going back upriver, no going on to the next landing. What "solved"
means is the encounter's own: usually winning, sometimes not (see the coal
miners).

## The caption system

**Sizes** (built): an **aside** over the speaker's cards, play going on
underneath; a **scene** from their cards down to the board; the **full**
table but the buttons, for introductions and lessons.

**Speakers** (built). The opponent's lines have the notch pointing up at their
portrait. Anyone else (Willie, Morag from the rail, the player) has their
name above the quote and their portrait in the corner of the caption.

**Closing** (built), in any combination:

- **Auto-close:** a countdown ring runs round the speaker's portrait; when it
  is full, the caption goes.
- **Manual:** tap the caption.
- **Replies:** one or two buttons with labels of their own (defaults *Agree* /
  *Decline*). Each leads to another caption, or to an effect. A single reply
  sits on the white side; the black one stays empty, with no pretend "No".
- **Compliance:** the caption waits for a move at the table. *"Throw away
  that four, child"*: the button stays disabled until you have tapped the four
  and only the four, and making the move closes the caption.

**Effects,** a short list that grows only when an encounter needs it: start
the match; add to the pot; the opponent folds before the deal (no ante paid);
give a snag; deliver a snag; turn the pot on (or the buttons into coins); set
or clear a flag; move or adjust money (IOUs).

**Flags.** A plain list of strings in the save, pushed when something starts
and removed when it resolves (`owesGaylord`, `promisedMorag`). No ledger
screen, no new UI.

**To build, from the tutorial play-through:**

- **Heavy buttons** (built, `src/ui/heavy.ts`). Only the moves that commit you
  to the hand (Call, Swap, Play hand) are held; everything else (Fold, Check,
  Skip, Next, Back, replies) is a light tap: giving up, or moving on, takes no
  time at all (← on the keyboard for the left button). Hold → or Space or Return for the white; `?quick` in
  development turns holding off. Each press soaks its own paper. A choice should feel decisive, not a brush against
  glass: hold to commit (0.9 s). The button soaks up ink like thin paper
  pressed into an inkpad, from a stylised fingerprint where the finger
  presses; let go early and it drains back, but the fingerprint lingers,
  fading: a patina on the controls. A haptic tick at the commit where phones
  allow it; Enter or Space held the same; screen readers activate at once.
  Only the action buttons; tapping cards and captions stays light.
- **Longer replies** (built: any number of replies; more than two, or any
  too long for half the row on this screen, stack up from the buttons, the
  last, white, nearest the thumb). Some replies don't fit half a row (*"No, ma'am"* is
  cut off on phones). A single reply may take the full width; and some
  conversations may want more than two replies, stacked. A compact game:
  desktop needn't look its best.

**How the player is addressed** is just text, by speaker and mood: Captain,
Cap'n, Herr Kapitän; Kitty's "Captain of my heart".

## Worked exchanges

### The stinking hides (replies, a branch, the captain's code)

1. A captain wants rid of a cargo of hides. *Agree* / *Decline*.
2. Decline → the player: *"Never! Your stinking hides would foul my ship."*
3. The captain: *"Captain's code, then. Beat me at Snag and I keep them; lose
   and they're yours. I'll put $5 in the pot."* *Agree* / *Decline*.
4. Agree → the match starts, $5 extra in the pot.
5. Decline → *"You refuse the code? Good day, then."* They fold before the
   deal (no ante), and the player is handed **Yellow-belly** (*does not honour
   the captain's code*).

### Gaylord's sponsorship (an obligation that comes back)

- Bored and flush, Gaylord invests "before the money is gone anyway": he pays
  the young captain's entry into a big table (a multi-captain game) for an IOU,
  $50 now, $100 back. Not a snag: an obligation (flag `owesGaylord`).
- Later he turns up to claim it: perhaps an expensive extension if the
  player's purse is thin, perhaps a best-of-three to settle it, or, destitute
  himself by then, asking for a loan on the same terms.
- Not adversarial, not generous either. The same shape serves other stories:
  promising Morag to look for Finlay, getting caught up in one of Kitty's
  schemes, or enlisting Max Moritz against the Walrus and the Inspector.

## Morag, the Governess

- A governess by trade and by temperament, on the river looking for her
  husband, **Finlay**, who has slipped off to play.
- She **loves** Snag Poker and is a master of it. What she cannot abide is
  everything that comes with it: the whisky, the wagering, the company.
- Author of a pamphlet on the subject (a candidate title: *On the Vileness of
  River Gaming*), which she quotes when it suits her.
- As a tutor: exacting, dry, secretly fond of a good pupil. Praise from her is
  rare and therefore worth something.
- She **is** the tutorial. In the story she probably returns as a passenger
  on the player's boat (name to be found), chasing Finlay downriver,
  disapproving all the way, quietly proud of you at the Fair.

## Landings (a dozen to aim for; six good ones will do)

Formats to mix: heads-up until the pot is won, best of three, table games.
Reasons to mix: to pass on a snag, to get passage, to get funds, to take on a
job.

**The route: up, then back down.** From Muscatine up to Dubuque for Pretty
Patty, then all the way down to St. Louis. Going up is the setup: slow
against the current and hungry for coal, the deck empty, Morag's lessons,
promises made and jobs taken on with a handshake. Coming down is the payoff:
Patty aboard, the current helping, and the same landings again, where the
people met on the way up remember you: debts called in, favours returned,
jobs to deliver. Two visits for one setting, as long as the second visit
changes something (the risk is that the way back feels like padding).

*Upriver:*

1. **Muscatine.** Father ashore; Morag's lessons begin. The harbour master
   won't clear the boat unless you beat him.
2. **The Tri-Cities** (Davenport, Rock Island, Moline). In spring 1904 nobody
   agrees how many cities they are: Gilbert has only just renamed itself
   **Bettendorf** (1903, after the brothers whose wagon works it lured from
   Davenport), and East Moline was incorporated the same year; "Quad Cities"
   only won out around 1930. So the local game runs until someone wins with
   **trips**, or with **quads**, depending on which side of the argument the
   table takes: the old guard plays to three, Bettendorf to four (and in
   Bettendorf they'll bet on anything, including which it'll be). Quads
   should stay a legend, not a requirement: a game played strictly until four
   of a kind could go on all night. The **railroad bridge** at Rock Island
   (the first across the Mississippi, 1856; Lincoln defended it in court
   after a steamboat hit it: from memory) is the queue: a line of boats, play
   the captains ahead for their place.
3. **The coal depot, and the striking miners** (below). Rock Island County
   was coal country: **Coal Valley**, a few miles south of Moline, was named
   for its mines, open from the 1850s and worked until 1942, all
   shallow room-and-pillar diggings in the Rock Island seam. The depot on the
   Rock Island or Moline levee, fed from Coal Valley (how the coal reached the
   river: to verify), struck. The boat burns through coal going upstream, so
   this is where it bites.
4. **LeClaire and the Rock Island Rapids.** Above the Tri-Cities, some 14
   miles of rock ledges; boats took on a specialist rapids pilot at LeClaire,
   a town that lived by the trade (from memory, to verify). **Kitty Sawyer**,
   the best pilot on the rapids, picks her clients at Snag Poker: she seats
   the captains waiting to go through at a High Rollers table and plays in it
   herself. She goes with whoever wins, or, if she wins it herself, with the
   runner-up. So you needn't win, only finish ahead of every other captain.
   (A woman pilot isn't a stretch: Mary Becker Greene was licensed on the Ohio
   in the 1890s; from memory.) Going back down, her table again.
5. **Dubuque.** Pretty Patty and her inventor (see The cargo).

*Downriver,* Patty aboard: LeClaire and the rapids, the Tri-Cities, the coal
depot and Muscatine again, each with something changed; then new river below
Muscatine (from memory, all to verify):

6. **Burlington** and **Fort Madison.**
7. **Keokuk**, where a canal with locks took boats round the Des Moines
   Rapids: **the queue at the lock**, a line of boats, the eggs going off.
8. **Hannibal**, Mark Twain's boyhood town (an obvious nod, and the cartoon
   would make the most of it; Kitty Sawyer is already one).
9. **Alton.**
10. **St. Louis**, under the Eads Bridge. Patty delivered; the Fair; the
    League opens.

Still to place, wherever they fit: **a nosy cargo inspector**; **the mail
bag** (another captain bound downriver could take it…); **Podunk Junction**.

## The river in segments

The river comes in stretches, each between two gates. Inside a stretch you
can go up and down as you please: to deliver a job, settle a snag, or try a
table again. The gates are where the river itself says no: you don't go back
down past the Rock Island Rapids until you've done what needs doing above
them, because of the pilot and the cost of climbing them again (it's hard
going, and the coal shoveller earns his keep that day). Almost open world,
tightly scoped and bite-sized: a few planned encounters and characters per
stretch, a few jobs and snags to resolve, and a gate to pass.

1. **Muscatine to the Tri-Cities.** Muscatine, the Tri-Cities, the coal
   yard. Visited going up and again coming down.
   *Gate:* **the Rock Island Rapids**: Kitty Sawyer's table, and coal enough
   to climb.
2. **Above the rapids: LeClaire to Dubuque.** Patty and the advance on her.
   More to find (Clinton, Bellevue: to look into).
   *Gate:* the rapids again, going down: Kitty plays for that too.
3. **Below Muscatine: Burlington to Keokuk.**
   *Gate:* **the Des Moines Rapids**: the canal at Keokuk, the queue at the
   lock.
4. **Keokuk to St. Louis.** Hannibal, Alton, St. Louis.

## Money is the fuel

Poker is the engine of the river; money is its fuel. The young captain is
underfunded: the jobs don't pay well enough to keep the boat going, so every
stretch costs more than the work brings in, and the difference is won at the
table. **One purse all the way down.**

- **Every leg has a price.** Coal, mostly: a dock will always sell it, at a
  premium. Also pilots, lock fees, harbour dues. To go on, you need the sum;
  the encounter that unlocks the next stretch has a stake you must afford.
- **Where the money comes from:** Father's starting cash in Muscatine; the
  **advance on Pretty Patty** in Dubuque; and until then, winnings, snag
  resolutions, odd jobs and small freight.
- **Side jobs:** play someone for the privilege of a well-paying job (a load
  that goes further up, or back down to settle a snag), and get paid enough
  for the next ante.
- **Never stuck.** Losing costs time, not the game: there's always an odd job
  at poor pay (carrying something, working aboard someone else's boat), so a
  thin purse is a detour, never a dead end.

## The grand steamers

The big passenger and excursion steamers moor for the night somewhere new
each evening, at a landing or out on the water, like cruise ships; smaller
boats tie up alongside, and the evening is cards, supper and whiskey aboard
the grandest of them. That's where the **High Rollers tables** are, and since
the steamers move, those tables turn up anywhere along the river.

- **Dress is the invitation.** Win a decent suit and waistcoat (at a table,
  or as a job's payment), and you're asked aboard. Wardrobe as progression.
- **Real flavour** (from memory, to verify): the **Diamond Jo Line**, run out
  of Dubuque, carried passengers between St. Louis and St. Paul until about
  1911; the **Streckfus** excursion steamers worked out of Rock Island. The
  riverboat gambler's heyday was before the Civil War, so by 1904 it's half
  legend, which suits a 1927 cartoon that romanticises it anyway.

## Who is where

- **Muscatine: Finlay**, "Carouser", carouses here and hereabouts, and
  **Morag** hunts him down. Passing Muscatine a few times (up, and down
  again) lets their story unfold in instalments: where he was seen, what he
  owes, how close she came.
- **Bettendorf: Gaylord Montague, Esq.** Where else would a card-sharp live
  than a town that bets on anything? Going up, **short of funds**, he offers
  the young captain a load to carry. Coming back down he wants **passage to
  St. Louis**, and plays for it: if he loses he pays the pot, if he wins he
  rides for free. Somewhere in between (before or after), he plays for **the
  loan** (Gaylord's sponsorship, below). He travels, too: he can turn up at
  any High Rollers table.
- **LeClaire: Kitty Sawyer**, the rapids pilot: her High Rollers table going
  up, and again coming down.
- **Anywhere a grand steamer moors: Eliza Bathory, "Bone Pale"**, no
  captain: a high roller at the grand steamers' tables, now and then. She plays for sport
  and has money to spare: she sets aside a sum to lose each night, and
  usually retires with the pot.

## The crew of the Best Intentions

A small sternwheel freighter, and she runs tight. By law (the federal
steamboat inspectors; from memory, to verify) a steamboat carried a licensed
master, pilot and engineer; on a boat this small the master is often the
pilot too, but the engineer is always his own man. Everyone else doubles up.
Names and personalities below are first drafts.

- **The captain: the player.** The deckhand made captain, and the clerk as
  well: the books, the purse, the freight bills are all his. No pilot's
  licence and no time to get one; the cartoon doesn't fuss, except where the
  river does: the rapids want a real pilot (Kitty).
- **Willie, deck hand and galley boy.** The narrator and the player's helper
  (roster.md): tips, captions, his reckoning on each captain. Lines, loading,
  and the crew's supper, which is mostly beans.
- **Steamboat Bill, "Father".** The old captain, ashore with family. May turn
  up again down the river.
- **The engineer: Augustus "Gus" Klemm.** From Davenport's Germans (a large
  community then). Meticulous, oil to the elbows, talks to the engine (who has
  a name of her own, *Old Hildegard*, and moods). Knows to the shovel what
  each stretch will burn: the voice of coal and the cost of going up. A steam
  man with a gasoline motor crated on deck: he scorns Pretty Patty in public
  and sneaks out at night to look at her.
- **The fireman: Lars Brekke.** Norwegian, enormous, nearly silent; eats
  enough for two and shovels enough for three. Sings while he works (a
  caption of musical notes; the tune is a folk song nobody else knows).
- **The second fireman, hired by the stretch.** For the hard going (up the
  rapids, against a spring flood), the captain takes on a second fireman at
  the landing: an expense, priced by the stretch. Some of them are the
  **Unionized Coal Shoveller**, the snag that already exists: he works to
  rule, breaks on the hour, and is a pleasure to pass on.

**A cook?** Not for four. With passengers aboard there are mouths enough,
and that can be a passenger's fare: someone who pays her passage in the
galley (below).

## Passengers

Rotating travellers, each with somewhere to be: they come aboard at one
landing and leave at another, and while aboard they're company at the rail,
a voice at supper, now and then an opponent. Passage is money, and the
boat needs money; some passengers are better company than fares, and some
are **snags** (the In-Laws, the Layabout Nephew and the Travelling Preacher
already are). First drafts:

- **Mrs. Hannelore Pfund**, a widow going down to her daughter in St. Louis.
  Once cooked on a packet boat; pays her fare in the galley, in pies and in
  opinions (on the beans, on poker, on Willie's posture). Morag's natural
  ally, or rival.
- **Ambrose Quill**, a drummer (a travelling salesman) with two sample cases,
  getting off at every other landing. Sells anything, plays poker eagerly
  and badly, and pays his debts in samples (a snag waiting to happen).
- **A clammer from Muscatine** with a pearl in his pocket. The pearl-button
  works paid for shells, but now and then a mussel held a freshwater pearl,
  and the upper Mississippi had a pearl rush round the turn of the century
  (from memory, to verify). He's taking his to sell in St. Louis and trusts
  nobody, least of all card players.
- **A young photographer with a Kodak Brownie** (new in 1900), bound for the
  Fair to photograph everything. Every landing ends in a group portrait
  (a frozen frame in the film); she wants a picture of Pretty Patty more than
  anything.
- **The Reverend**, going to the Fair to save souls on **the Pike** (its
  amusement street). Disapproves of the card table loudly, and asks
  quietly, later, how the betting works. The Travelling Preacher snag, met
  as a person first.
- **Honeymooners** going to the Fair, who argue about everything and are
  delighted with each other.
- **Morag**, chasing Finlay; **Gaylord**, coming down, if he wins his
  passage.

## A river of all sorts

The river in 1904 was anything but all white: Black rivermen worked every
boat, St. Louis had a large Black community and was the capital of ragtime,
the upper river was Sauk, Meskwaki and Ho-Chunk country within living
memory, and the towns were full of Germans, Irish, Scandinavians and
Bohemians. A cast with nobody but white faces would be its own distortion.
The trap runs both ways: put the only Black character on the coal pile and
it's the old caricature; leave everyone out to be safe and the river is
empty. Cartoons of the 1920s leaned hard on racial caricature (rivermen and
cooks among their targets); we take their drawing, music and timing, and
leave that out.

**Principles:**

- **Range, not a token.** Every group turns up more than once, across
  stations: captains and passengers, rich and broke, sharps and fools, crew
  and gentry. A Black cook is fine when she isn't the only Black person on
  the river.
- **The joke is the personality, never the origin.** Everyone is a
  caricature here, of a temperament (Willie's eagerness, Morag's rectitude,
  Gaylord's manners), not of a people.
- **Accents for all or none.** Morag's Scots, Lars's Norwegian, the
  engineer's German (or Scots): the captions give everyone their voice, and
  nobody's is the funny one.
- **Everybody wants something.** Their own goal, their own wit, the last
  word now and then. Nobody exists to serve someone else's scene.
- **Mind the drawing.** Rubber-hose cartoons grew partly out of minstrelsy
  (the white gloves, the wide mouths). The portraits draw everyone as a
  person with their own features, in the same style and with the same care.

**People to draw from** (first drafts):

- **The pianist on the grand steamers.** St. Louis was ragtime's capital
  (Scott Joplin wrote *The Cascades* for the 1904 Fair; from memory, to
  verify). A Black pianist who plays the High Rollers tables, knows every
  regular's tells, and is, in a silent film, the one supplying the music: the
  score is his. Sharp, unhurried, in on every joke.
- **A St. Louis businesswoman** heading home from a selling trip upriver: hair
  preparations, sold door to door and through agents (the flavour of Annie
  Turnbo Malone, whose Poro company began in St. Louis around 1902; fictional,
  not her). Shrewd, well dressed, a formidable card player: a match for
  Eliza at the grand tables.
- **Mrs. Pfund, or whoever pays her fare in the galley,** could be Black once
  the river has these others: her pride (a packet-boat cook, and the best on
  the river) and her opinions are what she's made of.
- **The second fireman,** hired by the stretch, is a different man each time:
  Irish, Black, Bohemian, a farm boy. Rotating, so no group is "the
  shoveller".
- **A Meskwaki passenger** going to the Fair, where its anthropology exhibits
  put living people on show (Geronimo among them; from memory). He's been
  asked to appear and is going to negotiate his fee: wry, practical, and
  better informed about the river than anyone aboard. No feathers, no
  mysticism. The Meskwaki had bought their own land at Tama, Iowa, in 1857
  (to verify).
- **The river's mixed families.** Antoine LeClaire, after whom LeClaire is
  named, was of French and Potawatomi descent (from memory). The rapids
  pilots could come from such families, Kitty Sawyer among them, if it suits
  her.
- **Others to place:** Bohemian Czechs (Iowa had them; Dvořák summered in
  Spillville in 1893), Irish, a Chinese American (not a laundry), Jewish
  merchants (never as the money-lender or the sharp).
- **Already in the gallery: Lenny "Troubadour" King**, a wandering
  troubadour with a cardboard violin (no captain, no boat), Jewish, and
  doesn't make a thing of it. His gloom is his own, not his people's.
- **The engineer: German or Scots?** A Scots engineer is a fine tradition:
  Kipling's *McAndrew's Hymn* (1894, free), the old engineer praying to his
  engines, and later Scotty. But Morag and Finlay are already Scots; a third
  could be the joke (Morag and the engineer arguing in a dialect nobody else
  aboard can follow), or one too many.

**A cartoonist worth remembering:** George Herriman, who drew *Krazy Kat*
(Ignatz is in the gallery), was a Creole of colour from New Orleans,
working in the very style we borrow.

**Settled in the roster:** James Robert "Jim-Bob" Lee read like an echo of
Robert E. Lee (also a famous steamboat, of the 1870 race against the
*Natchez*), and had little else going for him: retired.

## Characters with secrets

- **Max Moritz, Captain of the Mischief.** At the table, one very tall captain
  in a very long coat (the only hint: *"The coat giggles."*). Story Mode
  reveals the truth: Wilhelm Busch's two rascals, Max and Moritz, one standing
  on the other's shoulders. Once seated, one of them slips out under the table
  to swap cards and peek at hands. Their play style (the "griefer": not out to
  win, out to make you lose) fits; the reveal could come with a new rule for
  that match (they can see one of your cards, say), or simply as the joke.
- They **take turns on top.** Between rounds the coat changes hands: the
  portrait shows whichever boy is wearing the top half this round, and the
  play style follows him (one cautious, one reckless, say), while the other is
  off doing mischief under the table. One captain on the table, two
  personalities to read: attentive players can learn to tell them apart.

## Backlog

- **Portraits.** The letter in a circle is the biggest visual gap. First take
  stock of the roster and decide what to draw. Each captain gets at least three
  moods, **Neutral**, **Confident** and **Nervous**, shown as the hand goes, so
  a glance at a portrait is a read (a tell, and a bluff for the good ones).
  Except **Steamboat Bill**: the same doomed stare whatever his cards say.
